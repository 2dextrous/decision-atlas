<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="Floods, carbon rules and supplier misconduct are supply chain risks too." next="How it works" nextMinutes={3}>

ESG stands for environmental, social and governance. In supply chains, each part shows up as a concrete risk:

**Climate risk** comes in two forms:

- **Physical:** floods, heatwaves, cyclones and droughts that shut plants, warehouses or roads. A warehouse on a flood plain or a supplier in a cyclone-prone port city is exposed.
- **Transition:** costs from the move to a low-carbon economy, such as carbon pricing, efficiency rules, or the EU's carbon border adjustment (CBAM), which puts a carbon cost on imports like steel and aluminium. Customers' own net-zero targets also push suppliers to measure and cut emissions.

**Social risk:** unsafe working conditions, child or forced labour, or unfair wages at a supplier, often several tiers down. Beyond the ethics, the consequences include import bans, lost customers and reputational damage.

**Governance risk:** bribery, fraud, weak controls and non-compliance, at your company or at partners acting for you.

Management usually follows a familiar loop: **map** where exposure sits (sites, suppliers, routes, materials), **assess** it with climate data, audits, certifications and questionnaires, **act** through supplier codes of conduct, corrective plans, alternative sources and climate-proofing sites, and **report**. In India, the top 1,000 listed companies publish a Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report (BRSR), which includes value-chain information. Reporting frameworks such as the GHG Protocol and climate disclosure guidance shape what gets measured.

<KeyIdea>

Treat climate and ESG issues as supply chain risks: map exposure across sites and tiers, assess it with data and audits, act with suppliers, and report honestly.

</KeyIdea>

<Level n={2} title="How it works" minutes={3} teaser="Assessing physical climate risk site by site, a flood adaptation decision in rupees, and how social risk is checked in practice." next="Deep dive" nextMinutes={3}>

### Physical climate risk, site by site

Risk at a site combines three things:

- **Hazard:** how likely and how severe floods, heat, cyclones or droughts are at that location, today and under future climate scenarios.
- **Exposure:** what sits there: stock, equipment, the flows that depend on the site.
- **Vulnerability:** how badly the site is affected and how quickly it recovers (building height, drainage, backup power, alternative sites).

<Example title="a warehouse on a flood plain">

A Chennai warehouse has about a 10% chance each year of a flood that closes it for 2 weeks. It handles ₹5 crore of sales a week at a 20% margin.

- Impact of one flood: 2 weeks × ₹5 crore × 20% = ₹2 crore of lost margin.
- Expected loss: 10% × ₹2 crore = ₹20 lakh a year.
- Adaptation (raising racking, moving stock to higher locations before the monsoon, a standby site agreement) costs ₹8 lakh a year and cuts the impact by 70%, saving about ₹14 lakh a year in expected loss.

The adaptation pays, before counting customer goodwill.

</Example>

### Transition risk in numbers

Transition risk shows up as costs: a carbon price on emissions, carbon border charges on exports to the EU, higher costs for high-emission materials, or customers moving to lower-carbon suppliers. Modelling the effect of a carbon price on landed costs, as in carbon-aware network design, shows which products and routes are most exposed.

### Checking social risk

1. **Prioritize** suppliers by country, sector and spend, since some sectors and regions carry higher risks of forced or child labour or unsafe conditions.
2. **Self-assessment questionnaires,** followed by **audits** for higher-risk suppliers, often against recognized standards such as SMETA or SA8000.
3. **Corrective action plans** with deadlines, verified by follow-up.
4. **Grievance channels** that workers can use safely.
5. **Trace materials** to their origin where laws require it, such as cotton or polysilicon under forced-labour import rules.

<KeyIdea>

Assess physical climate risk through hazard, exposure and vulnerability, and compare expected losses with adaptation costs. Quantify transition risk as cost, and manage social risk through prioritized checks, corrective actions, grievance channels and traceability.

</KeyIdea>

### Frameworks converging on supply chains

- **ISSB standards (IFRS S1 and S2, 2023):** a global baseline for sustainability and climate disclosures, building on the earlier TCFD recommendations, including climate risks and Scope 3 emissions.
- **EU CSRD and its reporting standards:** detailed sustainability reporting for large companies, including value-chain information.
- **EU due diligence rules:** obligations to identify and address human-rights and environmental harms in supply chains.
- **India's BRSR:** SEBI's Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report for the largest listed companies, with a core set of indicators subject to assurance and growing expectations of value-chain disclosure.

For suppliers, this means customers will ask for emissions data, policies and evidence, and the questions will become more standardized.

### Double materiality

European reporting uses **double materiality**: a topic matters if it affects the company's finances (outside-in) or if the company's activities significantly affect people and the environment (inside-out). Supply chain teams contribute to both: climate risks to sites and suppliers, and the company's impacts through its suppliers' practices.

### The limits of audits

Social audits find some problems and miss others: they are announced in advance, they last a day or two, and they can be manipulated with false records and coached workers. Better results come from combining audits with worker voice (anonymous channels and surveys), long-term supplier relationships that support improvement, responsible purchasing practices (realistic prices and lead times, since squeezing suppliers can push them towards abuses), and traceability for high-risk materials.

### Greenwashing rules in India

Environmental claims are now regulated more tightly. The Advertising Standards Council of India issued guidelines on environmental claims in 2023, and the Central Consumer Protection Authority issued guidelines in 2024 on preventing greenwashing and misleading environmental claims. Claims such as "eco-friendly" or "carbon neutral" need specific, verifiable evidence. Supply chain data is often that evidence, so its quality has legal consequences.

<Trap>

Collecting supplier questionnaires and filing them is not risk management. Without verification, prioritization and follow-up on what they reveal, questionnaires create a false sense of security and can become evidence that the company knew and did nothing.

</Trap>

<KeyIdea>

ISSB, CSRD, due-diligence laws and BRSR are pushing ESG into supply chains. Apply double materiality, go beyond audits with worker voice and responsible purchasing, and back every environmental claim with verifiable supply chain data.

</KeyIdea>

<Q q="What is the difference between physical and transition climate risk?">

Physical risk is direct damage or disruption from climate events, such as floods, heat or storms. Transition risk is the cost of adapting to a low-carbon economy, such as carbon prices, new regulations, border adjustments and changing customer requirements.

</Q>

<Q q="How would you find forced-labour risk in a supply chain?">

Map suppliers beyond tier 1 for high-risk materials and regions, use risk data and certifications, run independent audits and worker interviews, require codes of conduct and traceability documents, and set corrective action plans, with alternative sources ready if problems aren't fixed.

</Q>
