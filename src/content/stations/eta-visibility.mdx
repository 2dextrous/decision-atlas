<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="From 'it left on Monday' to 'it will arrive at 4 pm, and here's why it might not'." next="How it works" nextMinutes={3}>

Once a shipment leaves, two questions matter: **where is it now**, and **when will it really arrive**?

**Visibility** answers the first. Data comes from GPS devices and telematics in trucks, carrier apps, milestone scans (picked up, departed hub, arrived, out for delivery), port and rail updates for containers, and sometimes toll or e-way bill records. Pulling these feeds together gives one live view of every shipment, instead of phoning drivers.

**ETA prediction** answers the second. The carrier's planned arrival time is often optimistic. A predicted ETA uses machine learning on history and live conditions:

- distance remaining and current speed,
- typical traffic for that route, day and hour,
- how long similar trucks usually wait at checkpoints, hubs and customer docks,
- weather, festivals and road disruptions.

Instead of one fixed time, a good model says "likely 4:10 pm, almost certainly before 6 pm", which is more honest and more useful.

The value is in acting early. If a truck carrying a big store order is predicted to be four hours late, planners can warn the store, re-sequence the dock schedule, or send a few cases from another warehouse before anyone complains. Customers get accurate "arriving at" messages instead of chasing updates.

<KeyIdea>

Visibility shows where shipments are; ETA prediction shows when they'll really arrive. Both pay off only when they trigger action before a delay reaches the customer.

</KeyIdea>

<Level n={2} title="How it works" minutes={3} teaser="How an ETA model adds what the map misses, the data that feeds it, and measuring whether ETAs are any good." next="Deep dive" nextMinutes={3}>

### From naive to predicted ETA

<Example title="a truck 300 km from its destination at 8 am">

- **Naive ETA:** 300 km at the current 45 km/h is 6.7 hours, arriving about 2:40 pm.
- **Historical speed:** trucks on this corridor at this time of day average 38 km/h, so 7.9 hours: about 3:55 pm.
- **Rest stop:** drivers on this route usually take a 30-minute break: about 4:25 pm.
- **Destination rules:** the city restricts heavy trucks until 9 pm, so the truck can't reach the warehouse before then: about 9:15 pm.

The planned 2:40 pm arrival was never possible. A model that knows the corridor, the driver patterns and the city's rules tells the warehouse in the morning, so it can schedule its dock and its labour for the evening.

</Example>

### What the model learns from

- **Trip history:** past trips on the same lane, with actual arrival times.
- **Live position and speed** from GPS devices, telematics or driver apps.
- **Patterns by time:** traffic by hour and day, festivals, weather.
- **Waiting times:** typical delays at toll plazas, checkpoints, hubs and customer docks.
- **Rules and constraints:** city entry restrictions, driver hours, delivery appointments.

Models such as gradient boosting learn how these combine, and quantile versions give a range (likely by 9:15 pm, almost certainly by 10 pm) instead of one time.

### Is the ETA any good?

- **Accuracy:** the share of arrivals within, say, an hour of the ETA, measured at fixed points before arrival (24 hours out, 4 hours out).
- **Bias:** do trucks arrive consistently later than predicted?
- **Calibration:** if the model says "90% sure by 10 pm", does that come true about 90% of the time?

### Acting on it

Predicted delays should trigger actions automatically or through a planner: rescheduling dock slots, informing customers with a new time, re-sequencing warehouse work, or sending stock from elsewhere for urgent orders.

<KeyIdea>

A predicted ETA adds what the map misses: real corridor speeds, breaks, waits and rules. Learn from trip history, give ranges, measure accuracy and calibration at fixed points, and connect delay predictions to actions.

</KeyIdea>

<Level n={3} title="Deep dive" minutes={3} teaser="Data gaps and consent, the yard and the dock, ocean visibility, control towers, and proving the value of visibility.">

### Data gaps and consent

Visibility is only as good as its data. GPS devices fail or get switched off, phone-based tracking depends on the driver's handset and network, and carriers share data unevenly. Combining sources (devices, apps, toll and e-way bill records through government platforms such as ULIP, carrier updates) fills gaps. Tracking drivers' phones needs informed consent, and India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 sets duties for handling such personal data.

### The yard and the dock

Many delays happen in the last few hundred metres: waiting at the gate, in the yard and at the dock. Visibility that stops at "arrived at customer" misses the hours spent waiting to unload, which drive detention charges and carrier dissatisfaction. Yard and dock scheduling systems, with appointment data, close that gap.

### Ocean visibility

For containers at sea, ship positions come from the automatic identification system (AIS) that vessels broadcast. Combining these with port congestion data, schedule changes and transshipment plans gives better arrival predictions than the shipping line's schedule. Cancelled sailings, port congestion and transshipment delays are common sources of surprise.

### From visibility to a control tower

A **control tower** combines visibility across shipments, orders and stock with rules and people who act on exceptions. The progression is the same as for any analytics: see what is happening, understand why, predict what will happen, and recommend or take action. Most of the value is in the last steps.

### Proving the value

Visibility projects should be judged on outcomes, such as:

- fewer customer calls asking "where is my order?",
- lower detention and demurrage charges,
- better dock productivity from accurate arrival times,
- fewer stockouts from late shipments caught in time,
- lower safety stock where lead times became more predictable.

<Trap>

A visibility dashboard that nobody acts on is a cost, not a capability. Define in advance which exceptions trigger which actions, who owns them, and how the result will be measured.

</Trap>

<KeyIdea>

Fill data gaps by combining sources and handle driver data with consent. Extend visibility to the yard, dock and ocean, build it into a control tower that acts on exceptions, and measure value in outcomes such as lower detention and fewer stockouts.

</KeyIdea>

<Q q="Why is a carrier's planned ETA often wrong, and how can you improve it?">

Planned ETAs assume ideal conditions and ignore typical delays like traffic, checkpoint queues and waiting at docks. A model trained on actual past trips, updated with live location and conditions, predicts much more realistic arrival times and their uncertainty.

</Q>

<Q q="How does better ETA prediction save money?">

Docks and labour can be scheduled to real arrival times, fewer emergency shipments are needed, customers can be warned or served from another location before a stockout, and less safety stock is needed when lead times become more predictable.

</Q>
