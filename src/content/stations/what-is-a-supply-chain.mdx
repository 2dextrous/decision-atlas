<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="Stages, three flows, and the line between planning ahead and reacting to orders." soon>

Think about a bottle of shampoo in a kirana store. Before it got there, a chemical supplier made the surfactants, a packaging supplier moulded the bottle, a factory filled and packed it, a warehouse stored it, a distributor delivered it, and the store put it on the shelf. All of those **stages**, plus the customer who buys it, form a supply chain.

Three things flow through it:

- **Product** flows mostly downstream, from suppliers to customers, and some flows back as returns.
- **Information** flows both ways: orders and sales data upstream, delivery plans and invoices downstream.
- **Money** flows upstream as each stage pays the one before it.

Supply chain management is coordinating all three so the customer gets the product when and where they want it, at the lowest total cost. The total matters, because a choice that saves money in one stage, such as huge factory batches, can cost more elsewhere, in warehouse space and stale stock.

One useful idea is the **push–pull boundary**:

- **Push** steps run ahead of demand, on forecasts: the factory makes shampoo before anyone orders it.
- **Pull** steps run in response to an actual order: a custom laptop is assembled only after you click "buy".

Where the boundary sits shapes everything: inventory is held just before it. Shampoo is pushed almost all the way to the shelf; a made-to-order sofa is pulled from much earlier.

<KeyIdea>

A supply chain is every stage involved in meeting a customer's need, linked by flows of product, information and money. Manage it for the total, not stage by stage.

</KeyIdea>

<SayIt>

A supply chain is all the stages involved in fulfilling a customer request: suppliers, manufacturers, distributors, retailers and the customer, connected by flows of product, information and funds. The goal is to maximize the total value created, not to optimize each stage separately. A key design choice is the push–pull boundary: steps before it run on forecasts, steps after it respond to actual orders, and inventory sits at the boundary.

</SayIt>

<Q q="What is the push–pull boundary, and where is it for a shampoo versus a custom laptop?">

It's the point where the supply chain switches from forecast-driven to order-driven work. For shampoo it's at the store shelf: everything up to there is made and moved on forecasts. For a made-to-order laptop it's at assembly: components are pushed to the factory, but final assembly happens only after the order.

</Q>

<Q q="Why optimize the whole supply chain rather than each stage?">

Because stages trade off against each other. A factory minimizing its own cost might run huge batches, which raises warehouse and obsolescence costs downstream. Only a total view finds the lowest overall cost at the service customers need.

</Q>
