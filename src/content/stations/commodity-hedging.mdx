<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="Trade some upside for certainty, so a price spike can't wreck the plan." soon>

Many products depend on raw materials whose prices swing: palm oil for soaps and snacks, crude-linked plastics for packaging, steel and aluminium for appliances, copper for cables. If palm oil jumps 30% in a quarter, a soap maker's margin can disappear before prices to customers can be raised.

**Hedging** fixes or limits the future price, so cost becomes predictable. Common tools:

- **Fixed-price supply contracts:** agree the price for the next 6 to 12 months with the supplier.
- **Forward contracts:** a private agreement to buy a set quantity at a set price on a future date.
- **Futures:** standardized contracts traded on exchanges, such as MCX in India or international metal exchanges, used to offset price moves in the physical purchase.
- **Index-linked contracts:** don't remove the swing, but make it transparent and shared.
- **Natural hedges:** for example, buying raw materials in the same currency you sell in.
- **Pre-buying stock** when prices are low, at the cost of holding inventory.

The key idea for interviews: **hedging reduces volatility; it doesn't promise a lower average price.** If prices fall after you've locked in, you pay more than the market. That's the price of certainty, not a mistake. Companies typically hedge a share of expected volume (say 50–70%) on a rolling basis, with clear limits so procurement doesn't drift into speculation.

<KeyIdea>

Hedging buys certainty, not profit. Lock in part of your exposure on a rolling basis so price shocks can't wreck the plan, and never let it become a bet on the market.

</KeyIdea>

<SayIt>

Commodity hedging reduces exposure to input price swings. Tools include fixed-price or index-linked supply contracts, forwards, exchange-traded futures, natural hedges and opportunistic pre-buying. The aim is predictable cost and protected margins, not beating the market, so I'd hedge a defined share of forecast volume on a rolling basis with clear governance, and link it to how quickly selling prices can be adjusted.

</SayIt>

<Q q="The company hedged palm oil and prices then fell. Was hedging a mistake?">

Not necessarily. Hedging trades upside for certainty: it protected the budget against a rise that could just as well have happened. Judge it on whether it reduced volatility as intended and stayed within policy, not on hindsight.

</Q>

<Q q="How much of the requirement would you hedge?">

Usually a portion, such as 50–70% of forecast volume, layered over several months. Hedging 100% risks being over-hedged if demand falls, and leaves no benefit if prices drop. The right share depends on forecast confidence and how quickly costs can be passed on to customers.

</Q>
