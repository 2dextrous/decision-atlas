<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="From statistical forecast to consensus plan to MRP input." next="How it works" nextMinutes={3}>

In the SAP world, demand planning usually happens in **SAP IBP (Integrated Business Planning)**, a cloud planning tool, while execution happens in **S/4HANA**. The flow mirrors the Demand line on this map:

1. **History in:** sales history and customer orders flow from S/4HANA into IBP.
2. **Clean the data:** pre-processing steps correct outliers and fill gaps, so a one-off bulk order doesn't distort the baseline.
3. **Statistical forecast:** IBP offers forecast models such as moving averages, simple, double and triple exponential smoothing (Holt-Winters), automated exponential smoothing, Croston's method for intermittent items, regression, ARIMA and gradient-boosted trees. Planners assign models by segment, or let the system choose.
4. **Measure the error:** IBP calculates error measures, such as MAPE and others, so planners can compare models and track accuracy.
5. **Consensus:** sales and marketing add their input in planning views, often in Excel, and the S&OP process agrees one demand plan.
6. **Short-term sensing:** demand sensing uses recent orders and other signals to adjust the next few weeks.
7. **Hand-off to execution:** the agreed forecast goes back to S/4HANA as **planned independent requirements (PIRs)**, the demand that MRP plans against (in S/4HANA they can also be maintained with transaction MD61).

From there, MRP takes over: it nets the PIRs against stock and open orders and creates planned orders and purchase requisitions.

The concepts don't change because the tool is SAP. Classification, method choice, bias and value-add all still apply; IBP just runs them at scale.

<KeyIdea>

IBP turns history into a statistical forecast, adds human input through consensus, and sends the agreed plan to S/4HANA as planned independent requirements, which MRP then plans against.

</KeyIdea>

### Planned independent requirements

A **planned independent requirement (PIR)** is a forecast quantity for a material, plant and period that MRP plans against. PIRs are created by IBP integration or entered with MD61 (changed with MD62, displayed with MD63). Each PIR carries a **requirements type**, derived from the material's **planning strategy**, which decides how PIRs and sales orders interact.

### Strategy 10 versus strategy 40

The strategy group is set in the material master's MRP 3 view.

<Example title="a month with a PIR of 1,000 and sales orders of 300">

- **Strategy 10 (make-to-stock, net requirements planning):** sales orders are delivered from stock and don't add to MRP's requirements on their own. The PIR of 1,000 drives production, and it is reduced when goods are issued for deliveries. Total requirement for MRP: 1,000.
- **Strategy 40 (planning with final assembly):** sales orders **consume** the PIR. The 300 ordered reduce the open PIR to 700, and the orders themselves become requirements. Total: 700 + 300 = 1,000. If orders reach 1,200, they exceed the forecast, and MRP plans for 1,200.

Without consumption, the forecast and the orders would be added together (1,300), double-counting the same demand.

</Example>

### Consumption mode and periods

For strategies that consume, the MRP 3 view also sets the **consumption mode** (backward, forward or both) and the number of days to look back and forward. A sales order for 15 June with backward consumption of 30 days can consume PIRs dated between 16 May and 15 June. Getting these right decides whether an order early or late in the month finds "its" forecast.

### How IBP sends its numbers

In IBP, demand planners work with **key figures** (actuals, statistical forecast, consensus demand) at planning levels such as product, customer, location and month. Forecast models are set up in steps: **pre-processing** (outlier correction, filling missing values), the **forecasting algorithms**, and **post-processing** (error measures). When the consensus is agreed, an integration job, typically through SAP's cloud integration services, sends the released demand to S/4HANA as PIRs at the right material, plant and period.

<KeyIdea>

PIRs carry the forecast into S/4HANA, and the planning strategy decides how sales orders interact with them. Strategy 40 consumes the forecast with orders (within the consumption window); strategy 10 doesn't. IBP sends its released consensus demand as PIRs.

</KeyIdea>

### Algorithms and drivers in IBP

Beyond smoothing methods and Croston for intermittent items, IBP offers regression with external variables and gradient-boosted decision trees that can use drivers such as price and promotions. Models are assigned to product segments, often based on ABC-XYZ or demand-pattern classification, and IBP can pick the best model automatically by comparing backtested errors. Forecast error key figures (MAPE, MAD, RMSE, bias and others) are stored so planners can compare models and track accuracy over time.

### Demand sensing

IBP's demand sensing uses recent orders and shipments, plus other signals, to adjust the forecast for the next few weeks, at a finer level such as daily or weekly by location. It sits on top of the monthly consensus, not instead of it, and should be judged at short lags against the plan it adjusts.

### Cleaning up old forecasts

PIRs dated in the past that were never consumed or reduced still look like demand to MRP, inflating requirements and creating unnecessary orders. S/4HANA provides reorganization transactions (MD74 to adjust requirements, MD75 to delete old requirements, MD76 to delete history) to clean them up. Running them regularly, or making sure the integration replaces old PIRs correctly, is part of good planning hygiene.

### Choosing a strategy by business type

<div class="table-wrap">

| Business | Typical strategy | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Make to stock, finished goods sold from stock | 10 | Forecast drives production; orders served from stock |
| Make to stock, sales orders should net against forecast | 40 | Orders consume forecast within the consumption window |
| Make to order | 20 | Each sales order drives its own production |
| Assemble to order with forecast-planned components | 50 or 70-type strategies | Components planned from forecast; final assembly waits for orders |
| Gross requirements planning | 11 | Plan production regardless of stock (e.g. to keep a line running) |

</div>

### From APO to IBP

Many companies used SAP APO Demand Planning (part of SAP SCM) for years. APO's mainstream maintenance also ends in 2027, so companies are moving to IBP, which runs in the cloud and covers demand, supply, inventory and S&OP in one model. The concepts (forecast models, consensus, release to execution) carry over; the data model, user interface and integration are new.

<Trap>

A wrong planning strategy is a common cause of inflated MRP demand: if sales orders don't consume PIRs when they should, forecast and orders add up, and the plant builds stock twice. Check the strategy group and consumption settings whenever MRP demand looks too high.

</Trap>

<KeyIdea>

IBP adds driver-based models, automatic model selection and short-term sensing. Clean up old PIRs so MRP doesn't plan for stale forecasts, choose planning strategies by business type, and expect APO users to move to IBP as support ends.

</KeyIdea>

<Q q="How does a forecast in IBP reach production planning in S/4HANA?">

The released consensus forecast is transferred to S/4HANA as planned independent requirements. MRP nets them against stock and open supply, explodes BOMs, and creates planned orders for production and purchase requisitions for bought materials.

</Q>

<Q q="Which IBP forecast model would you use for spare parts with sporadic demand?">

Croston's method, which is designed for intermittent demand, rather than standard exponential smoothing. I'd also check accuracy with measures that handle zero demand, and size stock from the demand distribution.
