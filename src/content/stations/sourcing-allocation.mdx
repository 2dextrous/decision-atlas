<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="One supplier is simpler, two is safer. Constraints decide the split." next="How it works" nextMinutes={3}>

**Single sourcing** gives a supplier all your volume. You get better prices, simpler coordination and a closer relationship, but if that supplier has a fire, a strike or a quality problem, you have nothing.

**Dual or multi-sourcing** spreads volume across suppliers. You get a backup that's already qualified and some competition, but smaller volumes per supplier, more management work, and sometimes slight differences in quality between sources.

Once you use several suppliers, you have to decide how to split the volume, and the cheapest supplier doesn't simply get everything. Suppose you need 50,000 units within 10 days:

| Supplier | Price | Capacity | Lead time |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | ₹100 | 30,000 | 7 days |
| B | ₹104 | 40,000 | 5 days |
| C | ₹98 | 20,000 | 15 days |

C is cheapest but can't deliver in time, so it's out for this order. A is next cheapest but can only make 30,000. So the best split is **A 30,000 and B 20,000**, at a total cost of ₹50.8 lakh. With hundreds of items, suppliers and constraints, this becomes a linear program: minimize total cost, subject to meeting demand, capacities, lead times and minimum order quantities, and often rules such as "no supplier gets more than 70%".

Companies frequently choose dual sourcing for high-spend or critical items, with an uneven split such as 70/30, so the second supplier stays qualified and ready to scale up if needed.

<KeyIdea>

Single sourcing buys efficiency; multi-sourcing buys resilience. When you split volume, let capacity, lead time and risk rules shape it, not just price.

</KeyIdea>

<Level n={2} title="How it works" minutes={3} teaser="Volume splitting as a linear program, how the answer changes with the deadline, and putting a price on disruption risk." next="Deep dive" nextMinutes={3}>

### The allocation model

- **Decision:** $x_s$, the quantity ordered from supplier $s$.
- **Objective:** minimize $\sum_s p_s\,x_s$, the purchase cost (or landed cost, if freight differs).
- **Constraints:** total quantity meets demand; each $x_s$ stays within capacity; suppliers that can't meet the deadline get zero; optional rules such as a maximum share per supplier or minimum order quantities.

Minimum order quantities and quantity discounts need yes-or-no variables, which makes it a mixed-integer program, still easy for a solver at this size.

<Example title="the 50,000-unit order, two deadlines">

Suppliers: A at ₹100 (30,000 capacity, 7 days), B at ₹104 (40,000, 5 days), C at ₹98 (20,000, 15 days).

- **Within 10 days:** C is excluded. A 30,000 + B 20,000 = ₹50.8 lakh.
- **Within 20 days:** C is allowed. C 20,000 + A 30,000 = ₹49.6 lakh, ₹1.2 lakh cheaper.

The deadline is worth ₹1.2 lakh here; if the business can wait ten more days, it saves that much.

</Example>

### Putting a price on single sourcing

<Example title="single or dual source for a critical part">

Annual volume 1 lakh units. Supplier X charges ₹100; supplier Y ₹103. Assume a 5% yearly chance that X is disrupted, which would cost ₹2 crore in lost production if there were no backup.

- **Single source (X):** purchases ₹1 crore, expected disruption cost 5% × ₹2 crore = ₹10 lakh. Total ₹1.10 crore.
- **Dual source (70% X, 30% Y):** purchases ₹70 lakh + ₹30.9 lakh = ₹1.009 crore. Y can ramp up if X fails, so the loss falls to, say, ₹50 lakh: an expected ₹2.5 lakh. Total about ₹1.034 crore.

Dual sourcing costs ₹90,000 more in purchases but saves about ₹6.6 lakh in expected disruption cost.

</Example>

The numbers depend on judgement about probabilities and impacts, but writing them down turns "is a second source worth it?" into a discussion of specific assumptions.

<KeyIdea>

Split volume with a cost-minimizing model that respects capacity, deadlines and share rules, and put an expected cost on disruption so the value of a second source can be weighed against its price premium.

</KeyIdea>

<Level n={3} title="Deep dive" minutes={3} teaser="What research says about mitigation versus contingency, when single sourcing works, parallel sourcing, and keeping a second source real.">

### Mitigation or contingency?

Tomlin (2006) compared ways of handling supplier disruptions: **mitigation** decided in advance (holding inventory, sourcing from two suppliers) versus **contingency** actions taken when the disruption happens (rerouting demand to a backup with spare capacity). The best choice depends on the disruption's character: frequent, short disruptions favour inventory; rare, long ones favour dual sourcing or contingent backups, because no affordable buffer lasts long enough.

### When single sourcing works

Single sourcing works best when the relationship is close and the network can respond. When a fire in 1997 destroyed the only plant making a critical brake valve for Toyota, the Toyota group's suppliers organized emergency production across dozens of firms and restored supply within days (Nishiguchi and Beaudet, 1998). The protection came from relationships, shared know-how and slack capacity in the network rather than from a second contract.

### Parallel sourcing

In **parallel sourcing**, each part has one supplier, but similar parts are spread across several suppliers. Each supplier gets the scale and commitment of single sourcing, while the buyer keeps competition (performance on one part affects the award of the next) and capable alternatives for emergencies. It is common in automotive.

### Keeping a second source real

- Order regularly from the second source, so its process stays proven and its people practised.
- Keep tooling, specifications and quality approvals current.
- Agree in advance how quickly it can scale up and at what price.
- Test it occasionally with larger orders.

A second source qualified years ago and never used is a document, not a backup.

### Splits in ERP

ERP systems implement splits with quota arrangements: each source gets a target share, and the system assigns new requisitions to the source furthest below its quota. The quotas should come from the allocation analysis and be reviewed when prices, capacity or risks change.

<Trap>

Splitting 50/50 by habit gives up the volume advantage of one supplier without capturing the price, capacity or risk logic behind a proper split. Set shares deliberately, and revisit them when conditions change.

</Trap>

<KeyIdea>

Match the protection to the disruption: inventory for short, frequent ones, second sources or contingent backups for rare, long ones. Single sourcing can work with strong relationships, parallel sourcing combines scale with competition, and a backup must be used to stay real.

</KeyIdea>

<Q q="You need 50,000 units in 10 days. A: ₹100, 30,000 capacity, 7 days. B: ₹104, 40,000, 5 days. C: ₹98, 20,000, 15 days. How do you split it?">

C is cheapest but its 15-day lead time misses the date, so it's excluded. Fill the next cheapest, A, to its 30,000 capacity, and buy the remaining 20,000 from B. Total cost: ₹30 lakh + ₹20.8 lakh = ₹50.8 lakh.

</Q>

<Q q="When is single sourcing the right choice?">

When the item is low-risk or the supplier relationship is strategic and deep, when volumes are too small to split economically, or when qualification is very costly. Even then, I'd keep a contingency plan: buffer stock, a pre-qualified alternative, or contract clauses on capacity and continuity.

</Q>
