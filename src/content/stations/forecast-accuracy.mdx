<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="How far off you are, which way you are off, and why the metric quietly shapes the forecast." next="How it works" nextMinutes={4}>

Two different questions hide behind "how good is the forecast?"

- **Accuracy:** how far off are we, ignoring direction?
- **Bias:** are we consistently too high or too low?

Bias is the more dangerous one. Random errors partly cancel out over time; a steady 5% over-forecast doesn't, and it quietly fills warehouses month after month. Report both, always.

The common accuracy measures each have a catch:

- **MAE** (average absolute error) is easy to read but in units, so you can't compare a fast mover with a slow one.
- **RMSE** squares errors before averaging, so it punishes big misses hardest. That is useful for inventory, because safety stock is driven by the size of the errors.
- **MAPE** (average percentage error) is the corporate favourite, and a trap. It can't handle zero sales, it over-weights tiny items, and it treats over- and under-forecasts unequally, which pushes models toward forecasting low.
- **WMAPE** (also called WAPE) divides total absolute error by total demand. Big items dominate, zeros are fine, and it's the practical default in many planning teams.
- **MASE** compares your error with the error of a naive "repeat last period" forecast. Below 1 means you beat naive; above 1 means you would do better doing nothing clever.

To track bias in production, use the **tracking signal**: the running sum of errors divided by the mean absolute deviation. If it drifts outside about ±4, errors are piling up on one side and the model needs attention.

And test properly. Never judge a model on the data it was fitted to; backtest on rolling periods it hasn't seen, at the horizon the decision actually uses.

<KeyIdea>

Report accuracy and bias together, prefer WMAPE or MASE to plain MAPE, and backtest at the horizon the decision uses.

</KeyIdea>

### One example, every measure

Define each week's error as forecast minus actual, so a positive error means over-forecasting.

<Example title="five weeks of one item">

<div class="table-wrap">

| Week | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Actual | 100 | 120 | 90 | 110 | 130 | 550 |
| Forecast | 110 | 110 | 105 | 100 | 120 | 545 |
| Error | +10 | −10 | +15 | −10 | −10 | −5 |
| Absolute error | 10 | 10 | 15 | 10 | 10 | 55 |

</div>

- **Bias (mean error):** −5 / 5 = −1 a week, or −0.9% of demand: almost unbiased.
- **MAE:** 55 / 5 = 11 units.
- **RMSE:** $\sqrt{(100 + 100 + 225 + 100 + 100)/5} = \sqrt{125} \approx 11.2$, a little above MAE because the 15-unit miss counts extra.
- **MAPE:** the average of 10/100, 10/120, 15/90, 10/110 and 10/130, which is 10.4%.
- **WMAPE:** 55 / 550 = 10.0%.
- **MASE:** if the naive "same as last week" forecast had an average error of 19 units, MASE = 11 / 19 = 0.58, so this forecast beats naive comfortably.
- **Tracking signal:** cumulative error ÷ MAE = −5 / 11 = −0.45, well inside ±4.

</Example>

### The formulas

$$
\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum \lvert e_t \rvert \qquad \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum e_t^{2}} \qquad \text{WMAPE} = \frac{\sum \lvert e_t \rvert}{\sum A_t}
$$

$$
\text{MAPE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum \frac{\lvert e_t \rvert}{A_t} \qquad \text{MASE} = \frac{\text{MAE}}{\text{MAE of the naive forecast}}
$$

Strictly, MASE scales by the naive forecast's average error on the training data, which makes it comparable across items and periods.

### Why MAPE leans low

MAPE divides by the actual. Forecast 150 when 100 sell and the error is 50%; forecast 100 when 150 sell and it is 33%, for the same 50-unit miss. A model or a planner judged on MAPE learns that forecasting low is safer, and the supply chain pays for it in stockouts. And a single week with zero sales makes MAPE undefined.

### Bias needs its own number

Accuracy measures ignore direction, so a forecast can look accurate while leaning one way. Track the mean error or the percentage bias, Σ(forecast − actual) ÷ Σ actual, by item group and by whoever adjusts the forecast, and watch the tracking signal for drift.

<KeyIdea>

Report accuracy and bias together. WMAPE and MASE are robust everyday choices, RMSE suits inventory, and MAPE needs a warning about zeros and its lean towards under-forecasting.

</KeyIdea>

### Each measure rewards a different forecast

The forecast that minimizes MAE is the **median** of possible outcomes; the one that minimizes RMSE is the **mean**. For symmetric demand they coincide. For skewed demand, which covers most slow movers and anything with occasional spikes, the median sits below the mean, so a model tuned to MAE forecasts low. If replenishment uses that number as the average rate, shelves run short. Choose the measure for the decision: mean-based measures for replenishment volumes, quantile measures for service-driven stock.

### Measure at the level and lag that matter

- **Level:** accuracy at brand level always looks better than at SKU-location level, because errors cancel when you add up. Report the level where stock is decided.
- **Lag:** use the forecast made one lead time before the period.
- **Weighting:** a plain average of item MAPEs treats a ₹100 item like a ₹1 crore item. Volume- or value-weighted measures reflect the money at stake.

### Checking prediction intervals

If you publish P10 and P90 forecasts, check them. Over a year, actual demand should land above the P90 in about 10% of periods. If it happens 25% of the time, the intervals are too narrow, and stock set from them will run short. Two properties matter: **calibration** (stated probabilities come true) and **sharpness** (intervals as narrow as calibration allows). Pinball loss scores both.

### How good is good?

There is no universal benchmark. A stable brand forecast monthly can be far more accurate than a fashion item forecast by store and week, and both can be well managed. The meaningful comparisons are against a naive forecast for the same items (MASE, forecast value add) and against your own history over time.

### Metrics get gamed

- **Accuracy = 1 − MAPE** can go negative, so it gets capped at zero, which hides the worst items.
- Rewarding planners on accuracy alone invites the safe, low number. Reward accuracy and bias together.
- Excluding "abnormal" periods from the metric is legitimate only if the rule was fixed in advance.

<Trap>

A forecast can show excellent accuracy at the total level while every SKU is wrong in compensating directions. Stock is held per SKU and location, so judge accuracy there too.

</Trap>

<KeyIdea>

Pick the error measure that matches the decision (mean, median or quantile), report it at the stock-holding level and decision lag, check that intervals are calibrated, and design metrics so nobody wins by forecasting low.

</KeyIdea>

</Level>

<Q q="What's wrong with MAPE?">

It divides by actual demand, so it's undefined when demand is zero and explodes for small items. It also weights small SKUs as much as big ones, and it penalizes over-forecasting without limit while under-forecasting is capped at 100%, which nudges models toward low forecasts and stockouts.

</Q>

<Q q="A forecast is accurate on average but the warehouse keeps overstocking. What's going on?">

Probably bias: small errors that are always in the same direction. Accuracy metrics hide it because they ignore the sign. Check the mean error and the tracking signal; a persistent positive bias means the forecast, or an override process, is systematically too high.

</Q>
