Two different questions hide behind "how good is the forecast?"

- **Accuracy:** how far off are we, ignoring direction?
- **Bias:** are we consistently too high or too low?

Bias is the more dangerous one. Random errors partly cancel out over time; a steady 5% over-forecast doesn't, and it quietly fills warehouses month after month. Report both, always.

The common accuracy measures each have a catch:

- **MAE** (average absolute error) is easy to read but in units, so you can't compare a fast mover with a slow one.
- **RMSE** squares errors before averaging, so it punishes big misses hardest. That is useful for inventory, because safety stock is driven by the size of the errors.
- **MAPE** (average percentage error) is the corporate favourite, and a trap. It can't handle zero sales, it over-weights tiny items, and it treats over- and under-forecasts unequally, which pushes models toward forecasting low.
- **WMAPE** (also called WAPE) divides total absolute error by total demand. Big items dominate, zeros are fine, and it's the practical default in many planning teams.
- **MASE** compares your error with the error of a naive "repeat last period" forecast. Below 1 means you beat naive; above 1 means you would do better doing nothing clever.

To track bias in production, use the **tracking signal**: the running sum of errors divided by the mean absolute deviation. If it drifts outside about ±4, errors are piling up on one side and the model needs attention.

And test properly. Never judge a model on the data it was fitted to; backtest on rolling periods it hasn't seen, at the horizon the decision actually uses.

<KeyIdea>

Report accuracy and bias together, prefer WMAPE or MASE to plain MAPE, and backtest at the horizon the decision uses.

</KeyIdea>

<Q q="What's wrong with MAPE?">

It divides by actual demand, so it's undefined when demand is zero and explodes for small items. It also weights small SKUs as much as big ones, and it penalizes over-forecasting without limit while under-forecasting is capped at 100%, which nudges models toward low forecasts and stockouts.

</Q>

<Q q="A forecast is accurate on average but the warehouse keeps overstocking. What's going on?">

Probably bias: small errors that are always in the same direction. Accuracy metrics hide it because they ignore the sign. Check the mean error and the tracking signal; a persistent positive bias means the forecast, or an override process, is systematically too high.

</Q>
