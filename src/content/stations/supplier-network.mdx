<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="The same network logic as warehouses, applied to the supply side." next="How it works" nextMinutes={3}>

Supplier network design applies network thinking to the supply side: which supplier sites should feed which of your plants, and how much should be sourced locally versus globally?

Take a two-wheeler maker with plants in Pune and Chennai, buying brake assemblies, wiring harnesses and plastic parts. For each part it weighs:

- **Landed cost:** price plus freight, duties, packaging and inventory.
- **Lead time and reliability:** a supplier 50 km away can deliver daily in small lots; one overseas needs weeks on the water and a big buffer of stock.
- **Capacity and capability:** can the supplier grow with you, and does it have the technology?
- **Risk:** geography, currency, tariffs, single-site dependence, and financial health.

Bulky, fast-moving or frequently changing parts usually favour **local suppliers** near the plant, sometimes in a supplier park right next to it. High-tech, low-weight parts with big economies of scale are often bought **globally** from a few specialist plants.

The network also has depth. Your direct (tier 1) suppliers depend on their own suppliers (tier 2, tier 3). Mapping those tiers often reveals that several "independent" tier 1 suppliers all rely on the same tier 2 plant, which is a hidden single point of failure.

Like warehouse network design, the decision can be modelled as an optimization: choose supplier sites and flows to minimize landed cost, subject to capacity, lead-time and risk constraints, then stress-test it against disruptions and tariff changes.

<KeyIdea>

Design the supply side like the distribution side: compare landed cost, lead time, capacity and risk for each supplier and plant pairing, and look beyond tier 1 for hidden dependencies.

</KeyIdea>

### Landed cost by pairing

For each supplier and each plant it could serve, add the price, freight, duties and packaging, and the inventory cost of the lead time.

<Example title="wiring harnesses for two plants">

Annual demand: Pune plant 60,000 harnesses, Chennai plant 40,000.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Supplier | Price | Capacity | Landed at Pune | Landed at Chennai |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| S1, near Pune | ₹1,000 | 70,000 | ₹1,010 | ₹1,060 |
| S2, near Chennai | ₹1,020 | 50,000 | ₹1,080 | ₹1,030 |
| S3, overseas | ₹900 | 100,000 | ₹1,040 | ₹1,040 |

</div>

S3's landed cost includes ₹120 of freight and duty and ₹20 of extra inventory for its long lead time.

Cheapest assignment: S1 supplies Pune (60,000 at ₹1,010) and S2 supplies Chennai (40,000 at ₹1,030), a total of ₹10.18 crore a year. S3 has the lowest quote and supplies nothing.

S3 is still useful: if S2 were disrupted, S3 could supply Chennai for only ₹10 a unit more, which makes it a sensible qualified backup.

</Example>

### Local suppliers and inbound flow

Close suppliers enable practices distant ones can't:

- **Frequent small deliveries** (several a day), keeping line-side stock low.
- **Just-in-sequence** delivery, where parts arrive in the exact order of the vehicles on the line (seats, dashboards, wiring).
- **Milk runs:** one truck collects from several nearby suppliers on a fixed loop, giving frequent deliveries without many part-empty trucks.
- **Supplier parks:** key suppliers located next to the assembly plant.

### Modelling it

At scale this is an optimization like distribution network design, applied upstream: choose supplier sites and flows to minimize landed cost, within capacities, lead-time limits and rules such as a minimum share for a second source, and then test it against scenarios like a tariff change or a supplier outage.

<KeyIdea>

Compare suppliers by landed cost for each plant, not by quoted price. Optimize the assignment within capacities, keep cheap-to-switch alternatives as backups, and use local suppliers for frequent, sequenced deliveries.

</KeyIdea>

### Concentration in the lower tiers

A network that looks diverse at tier 1 can depend on a handful of tier 2 or tier 3 sources: one resin producer, one chip foundry, one specialty chemical plant. Network design that stops at tier 1 misses the true single points of failure. For critical categories, map deeper, and treat a shared sub-supplier as a risk to model, not a detail.

### Regionalization and local-content rules

Several forces push supply networks towards regions:

- **Trade policy:** tariffs and export controls change landed costs and access.
- **Local-content incentives:** schemes such as India's production-linked incentives and phased manufacturing programmes reward local value addition.
- **"China plus one":** many companies have added suppliers in other countries, including India, to reduce concentration.
- **Speed:** shorter supply lines support faster product changes and lower inventory.

Regional networks usually cost more per unit than the single cheapest global source, so the business case rests on lower risk, inventory and lead time, and sometimes on access to incentives.

### Design decisions shape the supply base

Engineering choices decide how many suppliers a company needs and how replaceable they are. Common parts across products and plants concentrate volume and simplify sourcing; standard specifications allow more suppliers to compete; proprietary components lock in one source. Involving procurement early in design avoids accidental single sources.

### Resilience as a design goal

Resilient supply networks are designed, not hoped for:

- qualified alternatives for critical parts, ideally in different regions,
- flexibility to move volume between suppliers and plants,
- buffer stocks placed where recovery would be slowest,
- visibility of supplier capacity and status.

Each costs money in normal times, so the design should be explicit about which risks it protects against and at what price.

<Trap>

Comparing suppliers on ex-works prices and choosing the cheapest global source for bulky or fast-changing parts ignores freight, duties, inventory and responsiveness. Landed cost and lead time often favour the nearer supplier.

</Trap>

<KeyIdea>

Look below tier 1 for hidden concentration, weigh regional supply against its higher unit cost with risk, inventory and incentives in view, involve procurement in design choices, and pay for resilience deliberately where recovery would be slow.

</KeyIdea>

<Q q="Why might a manufacturer pay more to source a part locally?">

Shorter lead times mean less inventory and faster reaction to design or demand changes, freight is lower for bulky parts, and the supply is less exposed to ports, currency and tariffs. The total landed and risk-adjusted cost can be lower even when the unit price is higher.

</Q>

<Q q="What does mapping tier 2 suppliers usually reveal?">

Hidden concentration: several tier 1 suppliers depending on the same sub-supplier, region or raw material. That's a single point of failure that tier 1 diversification doesn't fix.

</Q>
