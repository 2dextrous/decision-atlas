<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="Centre, spread, outliers and whether the pattern stays put." next="How it works" nextMinutes={3}>

Most supply chain analysis rests on a few statistical ideas.

**Centre: mean or median?** Eleven days of sales for one item: 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 and 45. The mean is about 16.8, pulled up by one bulk order of 45. The median, the middle value, is 14 and better describes a normal day. When data has spikes, look at both.

**Spread:** the **standard deviation** measures how far values typically stray from the mean. The **coefficient of variation** (standard deviation ÷ mean) makes spread comparable across items with different volumes, which is why it's used for XYZ classification.

**Box plots and outliers:** a box plot shows the middle half of the data (from the first quartile Q1 to the third quartile Q3) as a box, with the median inside. The box's height is the **interquartile range (IQR)**. A common rule flags values beyond 1.5 × IQR outside the box as outliers. For the sales above, Q1 ≈ 12 and Q3 ≈ 17, so IQR ≈ 5 and anything above about 24.5 is flagged: the 45 stands out. Other methods include z-scores (beyond ±3 standard deviations) and robust versions based on the median.

An outlier isn't automatically an error. A one-off bulk order should be removed before forecasting baseline demand; a genuine new trend shouldn't.

**Stationarity:** a time series is **stationary** when its average level and its variability stay roughly constant over time. Many forecasting models assume it. Trends and seasonality break it, so analysts plot rolling averages and rolling standard deviations, use formal tests such as the augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test, and transform the data, by differencing or taking logs, until it's stable.

**Univariate vs multivariate:** univariate analysis looks at one variable, like sales history alone; multivariate looks at several together, such as sales, price and promotions. Remember that correlation isn't causation.

<KeyIdea>

Know the centre (and whether spikes distort it), the spread, which points are unusual and why, and whether the pattern is stable over time. Those four checks come before any model.

</KeyIdea>

### One spike, many effects

Take the eleven days of sales from the gist: 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 and 45.

<Example title="what the 45 does to the numbers">

<div class="table-wrap">

| Measure | With the 45 | Without it |
|---|---|---|
| Mean | 16.8 | 14.0 |
| Standard deviation | 9.66 | 2.58 |
| Coefficient of variation | 0.58 (class Y) | 0.18 (class X) |

</div>

- **The z-score test misses it:** $(45 - 16.8)/9.66 \approx 2.9$, under the usual cut-off of 3, because the spike has inflated the standard deviation it is measured against. This is called **masking**.
- **A robust test catches it:** use the median (14) and the median absolute deviation (MAD). The absolute deviations from 14 have a median of 2, so the robust z-score is $0.6745 \times (45 - 14)/2 \approx 10.5$, far beyond any threshold.
- **One spike changes the class:** the item's coefficient of variation jumps from 0.18 to 0.58, moving it from X (steady) to Y (variable) and inflating its safety stock.

</Example>

The lesson: check for outliers with robust measures (median, MAD, IQR), decide whether each one is a real part of demand, and only then compute the statistics that drive policies.

### Relationships: correlation and regression

Weekly sales of a product at five price points:

<div class="table-wrap">

| Price (₹) | 100 | 95 | 90 | 85 | 80 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | 200 | 215 | 236 | 250 | 270 |

</div>

A straight-line fit gives sales ≈ 549.2 − 3.5 × price, so each ₹1 cut adds about 3.5 units a week. The correlation is −0.998, very strong. At the average price and sales, the **price elasticity** is −3.5 × 90 / 234.2 ≈ −1.35: a 1% price cut raises sales by about 1.35%.

### Distributions that come up again and again

- **Normal:** sums of many small effects, such as demand over a lead time for steady items. The basis of z-values in safety stock.
- **Poisson:** counts of rare, independent events, such as spare-part demand or breakdowns.
- **Lognormal and other skewed shapes:** lead times and repair times, which can't go below zero but have long right tails.

<KeyIdea>

Spikes distort the mean, the standard deviation and every policy built on them, and can even hide from the z-score test. Use robust measures to find outliers, regression to quantify relationships such as price elasticity, and distributions that match the data's shape.

</KeyIdea>

</Level>

### Testing stationarity

Two common tests have opposite starting assumptions, which is why they are often used together:

- **ADF (augmented Dickey-Fuller):** assumes the series has a unit root (it wanders without returning to a mean). A small p-value rejects that, suggesting stationarity.
- **KPSS:** assumes the series is stationary. A small p-value rejects that, suggesting it isn't.

If ADF says stationary and KPSS agrees, fine; if they disagree, look at the plot and consider trend-stationary versus difference-stationary behaviour. Fixes include **differencing** (working with changes rather than levels), **seasonal differencing** (this month minus the same month last year) and **log or Box-Cox transforms** to stabilize variance that grows with the level.

### Autocorrelation

The **autocorrelation function (ACF)** shows how strongly a series is related to its own past values at each lag. A spike at lag 12 in monthly data signals yearly seasonality; slowly decaying autocorrelation suggests a trend or a unit root. The partial autocorrelation (PACF) helps choose ARIMA model orders. Autocorrelated forecast errors are a sign that a model has missed a pattern.

### Correlation traps

- **Spurious correlation:** two series that both trend over time (say, sales of a product and the population of a city) will correlate strongly even with no link. Granger and Newbold (1974) showed how regressions between unrelated trending series produce misleadingly "significant" results. Difference the series or model the trend first.
- **Simpson's paradox:** a pattern in the total can reverse within every group. Overall, faster carriers might seem to have more damage, but within each product type they have less, because they carry more fragile goods. Always check results within relevant segments.
- **Causation:** a correlation between promotions and sales doesn't measure the promotion's effect if promotions were run when sales were expected to rise anyway. Controlled experiments or careful causal methods are needed.

### Significant versus important

With enough data, tiny effects become statistically significant: a picking method that is 0.3% faster may pass a test and still not matter. Report the size of effects with confidence intervals, and judge them against what they are worth in practice.

<Trap>

Deleting every point flagged as an outlier removes real demand: festival spikes, bulk orders from genuine customers, the start of a new trend. Investigate each one, remove only true errors or one-off events from the baseline, and model the rest.

</Trap>

<KeyIdea>

Use ADF and KPSS together and transform series until they are stable, read the ACF for trend and seasonality, beware spurious correlation and Simpson's paradox, and judge effects by their size, not just their significance.

</KeyIdea>
