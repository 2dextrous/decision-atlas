<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="Two questions, how much it matters and how risky it is to get, give four strategies." next="How it works" nextMinutes={3}>

Not everything a company buys deserves the same treatment. Peter Kraljic's 1983 matrix sorts purchases on two questions:

- **Profit impact:** how much money flows through this category, and how much it affects product cost and quality?
- **Supply risk:** how hard is it to get: few suppliers, scarce materials, long lead times, specialist know-how?

That gives four boxes, each with its own strategy:

| | Low supply risk | High supply risk |
|---|---|---|
| **High profit impact** | **Leverage** (standard packaging, common chemicals): many suppliers, so compete them through tenders and auctions, consolidate volume and negotiate hard | **Strategic** (a key active ingredient, an advanced chip): build long-term partnerships, plan jointly, share forecasts and manage risk together |
| **Low profit impact** | **Non-critical** (office supplies, cleaning materials): automate buying with catalogues and purchasing cards and spend as little effort as possible | **Bottleneck** (a special spare part from one source): secure supply with safety stock, backup suppliers or redesign to remove the dependency |

The common mistake is using one approach everywhere: hammering a strategic supplier on price can damage a relationship you depend on, while building partnerships for office paper wastes time. Categories also move between boxes, for example when a single-source supplier gets a competitor, or when a commodity suddenly becomes scarce.

<KeyIdea>

Match the buying strategy to the category: compete leverage items, partner on strategic ones, secure bottlenecks and automate the non-critical.

</KeyIdea>

### Scoring the two axes

**Profit impact** combines measures such as:

- annual spend and its share of product cost,
- the category's effect on product quality or differentiation,
- how much a price change would move margins.

**Supply risk** combines:

- the number of qualified suppliers and how concentrated the market is,
- the time and cost to switch,
- availability of substitutes,
- lead times, and geographic or political exposure.

Score each on a common scale (say 1 to 10), with written definitions so different buyers score the same way.

### Tactics by quadrant

<div class="table-wrap">

| Quadrant | Goal | Tactics |
|---|---|---|
| Leverage | Lowest total cost | Tenders and auctions, volume consolidation, shorter contracts, index-linked prices |
| Strategic | Secure supply and joint value | Long-term agreements, joint planning, shared forecasts, co-development, executive relationships |
| Bottleneck | Continuity | Safety stock, alternative suppliers, standardizing the specification, long-term supply agreements |
| Non-critical | Low process cost | Catalogues, purchasing cards, bundling under one supplier, automation |

</div>

### Who has the power?

Kraljic's method also asks about the balance of power inside each quadrant. A strategic category where you are a big customer to the supplier is different from one where you are a small account. With buyer power, you can **exploit** it (push price and terms); when it is balanced, you **balance** it (partner); with supplier power, you **diversify** (develop alternatives, redesign).

<KeyIdea>

Score categories on profit impact and supply risk with written definitions, place them in quadrants, apply the matching tactics, and check the balance of power before choosing how hard to push.

</KeyIdea>

</Level>

### Moving a category, not just managing it

The best category strategies change the category's position:

- **Bottleneck to non-critical:** replace a special part with a standard one available from many suppliers.
- **Strategic to leverage:** develop or qualify new suppliers, or redesign to remove a proprietary input.
- **Leverage to strategic,** deliberately: concentrate volume with a partner in exchange for innovation or priority capacity.

### How suppliers see you

The supplier's view matters as much as yours. **Supplier preferencing** models (described by Steele and Court) classify customers by the account's value to the supplier and how attractive it is to serve. A supplier treats a large, attractive customer as core and protects it; a small, difficult account may be exploited or quietly dropped when capacity is short. A buyer that is "strategic" to its supplier but a nuisance account in return should expect weaker priority in a shortage.

### When the matrix oversimplifies

Gelderman and van Weele (2005) review common criticisms:

- Two dimensions hide many factors, and the scoring can be subjective.
- Categories contain items with very different risks.
- Interdependence between buyer and supplier, and long-term relationships, aren't captured well.

The matrix is a starting point for a conversation, not a calculation that decides strategy on its own.

### Positions change with the market

In 2021, semiconductors for cars looked like leverage or non-critical items for many buyers: cheap per unit, bought through tier 1 suppliers. The shortage turned them into bottleneck or strategic items overnight, and car makers that had treated them transactionally lost production. Re-assessing supply risk regularly, especially for low-spend inputs with concentrated supply, is part of the method.

<Trap>

Placing categories by spend alone puts every cheap item in the low-impact half and hides bottlenecks. A ₹500 part from a single source can stop a ₹50 lakh production line; supply risk must be assessed separately from spend.

</Trap>

<KeyIdea>

Use the matrix to move categories as well as manage them, consider how attractive you are to each supplier, treat the scoring as a conversation starter, and reassess supply risk regularly, because markets reshuffle quadrants.

</KeyIdea>

<Q q="A component costs little but has only one qualified supplier. Which quadrant, and what would you do?">

Bottleneck. The priority is continuity, not price: hold safety stock, qualify a second supplier, sign longer agreements to secure capacity, or redesign the product to use a standard part.

</Q>

<Q q="Why not run reverse auctions for every category?">

Auctions work for leverage items with many capable suppliers and clear specifications. For strategic items they can damage relationships, cut supplier investment and raise risk; for bottleneck items there may be no competition to exploit.

</Q>
