<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="One number that shows how much of a line's time turns into good output." next="How it works" nextMinutes={3}>

**OEE (overall equipment effectiveness)** asks a simple question: of the time a line was scheduled to run, how much turned into good product at full speed? It multiplies three factors:

$$
OEE = \text{Availability} \times \text{Performance} \times \text{Quality}
$$

Take one 8-hour shift (480 minutes) on a filling line rated at 100 bottles an hour, one every 0.6 minutes:

- **Availability:** 48 minutes were lost to a breakdown and a changeover, so it ran 432 of 480 minutes: **90%**.
- **Performance:** at full speed, 432 minutes would give 720 bottles. It made 648 because of minor stops and slow running: **90%**.
- **Quality:** 616 of the 648 bottles were good, the rest underfilled or mislabelled: **95%**.

OEE = 0.90 × 0.90 × 0.95 ≈ **77%**. Put simply, the line produced 77% of the good output it could have made in that shift.

The breakdown is more useful than the headline. Low availability points to breakdowns and long changeovers; low performance to small stoppages and slow speeds; low quality to defects and rework. These are often grouped as the "six big losses" of total productive maintenance (TPM). An often-quoted world-class benchmark is about 85%, but a line's own trend matters more than comparisons across plants.

Don't confuse OEE with **utilization**. A line can be busy 95% of the time and still have poor OEE if it runs slowly and makes scrap.

<KeyIdea>

OEE turns lost time, lost speed and lost quality into one number. Improve the factor that's lowest, and improve the bottleneck first, because that's where lost minutes cost the whole plant.

</KeyIdea>

### OEE as a time model

OEE is easiest to understand as time slipping away in stages:

- **Planned production time:** the time the line was scheduled to run.
- **Run time:** planned time minus stops (breakdowns, changeovers). Availability = run time ÷ planned time.
- **Net run time:** the time the actual output would take at the ideal rate. Performance = net run time ÷ run time.
- **Fully productive time:** the time the *good* output would take at the ideal rate. Quality = good units ÷ total units.

OEE = fully productive time ÷ planned production time, which is the same as multiplying the three factors.

<Example title="the filling line's shift in minutes">

Planned 480 minutes, ideal rate one bottle every 0.6 minutes.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Stage | Minutes | Loss |
|---|---|---|
| Planned production time | 480 | |
| Run time | 432 | 48 min of breakdown and changeover |
| Net run time (648 bottles × 0.6) | 388.8 | 43.2 min of small stops and slow running |
| Fully productive time (616 good × 0.6) | 369.6 | 19.2 min making bad bottles |
| **OEE** | **77%** | 369.6 ÷ 480 |

</div>

Seen as minutes, the losses can be compared directly: breakdowns and changeovers cost the most here, then speed losses, then quality.

### The six big losses

| Factor | Losses |

| Availability | Breakdowns; set-ups and adjustments |

### OEE, utilization and TEEP

OEE measures how well the *planned* time is used. **TEEP** (total effective equipment performance) also counts the time the line wasn't planned to run: TEEP = OEE × (planned time ÷ calendar time). If the line runs two shifts out of three, TEEP = 77% × 16/24 ≈ 51%. TEEP shows how much hidden capacity exists before buying new equipment: often a third shift or weekend work before new capital.

<KeyIdea>

OEE is fully productive time divided by planned time: availability, performance and quality losses expressed in one currency, minutes. The six big losses tell you where they come from, and TEEP adds the time the line was never planned to run.

</KeyIdea>

### The ideal rate decides everything

Performance compares actual output with an **ideal cycle time**. Choose it badly and OEE means nothing:

- Use the equipment's true best rate (design speed or the best demonstrated rate), not a slow "standard" rate. With a padded standard, performance can exceed 100% while real losses stay hidden.
- Use the same rule across lines and keep it stable, or trends become meaningless.

### How OEE gets inflated

- **Excluding stops from planned time:** if changeovers, cleaning or meetings are counted as "unplanned time", availability rises without any improvement.
- **Counting reworked units as good:** quality looks better while rework consumes capacity.
- **Manual data:** short stops of a minute or two are rarely written down, so performance losses vanish. Automatic counting on the line usually reveals far more small stops than anyone recorded.

### Where to use it

OEE matters most at the bottleneck, where every lost minute is lost plant output. Pushing OEE up on non-bottleneck machines may only produce more work in process. Comparing OEE between plants or companies is risky because definitions differ; a line's own trend, with a fixed definition, is far more useful. The often-quoted world-class figure of about 85% traces back to Nakajima's work on TPM, built from roughly 90% availability, 95% performance and 99.9% quality.

### The improvement sequence

1. Measure losses reliably, ideally automatically.
2. Attack the largest loss on the bottleneck first: often changeovers (SMED) or breakdowns (maintenance).
3. Standardize the fix, then move to the next loss.
4. Involve operators: autonomous maintenance and quick problem-solving at the line fix many small stops.

<Trap>

An OEE target without a fixed definition invites gaming. Agree the ideal rate, what counts as planned time and what counts as good before setting targets, and audit the data source.

</Trap>

<KeyIdea>

OEE is only as honest as its ideal rate and its definition of planned time. Use it mainly at the bottleneck, track trends rather than cross-plant comparisons, and attack the largest measured loss first.

</KeyIdea>

<Q q="Availability 90%, performance 90%, quality 95%. What is the OEE, and what would you look at first?">

About 77%. Availability and performance are the bigger losses here. I'd break down the downtime into breakdowns versus changeovers, and look at minor stops and speed losses, especially if this line is the bottleneck.

</Q>

<Q q="Is a machine with 95% utilization performing well?">

Not necessarily. Utilization only says it was busy. If it ran slowly or produced scrap, OEE could be much lower. And utilization on a non-bottleneck machine can be pointless, because it just builds inventory the bottleneck can't process.

</Q>
