<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="P10, P50 and P90: what they mean and why inventory needs them." soon>

A normal forecast says "next week's demand will be 10,000 units." That single number hides the part planners care about most: how far off it could be. A **probabilistic forecast** gives the range instead:

| Quantile | Meaning | Forecast |
|---|---|---|
| P10 | 10% chance demand is lower than this | 8,000 |
| P50 | the middle: as likely above as below | 10,000 |
| P90 | 90% chance demand is at or below this | 13,000 |

This matters because stock isn't set from the middle of the range. It is set from the tail. If you want to avoid running out 90% of the time, you need roughly the P90 number on hand, not the P50. Plan for 10,000 and you'll be short fairly often; plan for 13,000 and you're protected, at the cost of more stock. The forecast now lets the business choose its risk openly.

It also helps where the usual safety-stock formula struggles. That formula assumes demand follows a neat bell curve. Real demand is often skewed, with a hard floor at zero and occasional big spikes, especially for slow movers. A quantile forecast reads the tail from the data instead of assuming its shape.

Quantile forecasts are scored with **pinball loss**, which penalizes errors unequally on purpose. For a P90 forecast, being too low costs nine times more than being too high, which mirrors how a stockout usually hurts more than a little extra stock.

<KeyIdea>

A point forecast tells you the middle; inventory decisions live in the tails. Forecast the quantile that matches your service target.

</KeyIdea>

<SayIt>

A probabilistic forecast gives a distribution of possible demand, usually as quantiles like P10, P50 and P90, instead of one number. Inventory is set from the tail, not the middle: for a 95% service target you stock towards the P95 of lead-time demand. It also handles skewed and intermittent demand better than assuming a normal distribution, and quantile forecasts are evaluated with pinball loss rather than MAPE.

</SayIt>

<Q q="Your P50 is 10,000 and P90 is 13,000. How much do you stock?">

It depends on the service target and the cost of each mistake. For about 90% protection, stock near 13,000. If leftovers are cheap and stockouts expensive, go higher; if stock perishes, the newsvendor critical ratio tells you which quantile to aim for.

</Q>

<Q q="Why not just use the point forecast plus a safety-stock formula?">

That works when forecast errors are roughly normal. Many items, especially slow movers and promoted items, have skewed demand with spikes, where the normal assumption sets the buffer wrong. A quantile forecast reads the tail directly from the data.

</Q>
