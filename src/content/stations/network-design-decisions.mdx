<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="Four long-term decisions, and the one dial that trades cost against service." soon>

Network design sets the physical skeleton of a supply chain: plants, warehouses and the lanes between them. These are long-term, expensive decisions. A warehouse lease or a new plant locks a company in for years, and it decides most of what transport and inventory will cost afterwards.

It comes down to four decisions:

1. **Facility role:** what each site is for. Is a plant a low-cost producer, the hub for a region, or the lead site for new products?
2. **Location:** where plants and warehouses should be.
3. **Capacity:** how big each one should be.
4. **Market allocation:** which plant supplies which warehouse, and which warehouse serves which customers.

Most of the trade-offs move with one dial: **the number of facilities**. Add warehouses and:

- delivery gets faster and last-mile freight falls, because you're closer to customers;
- inventory rises, because each site needs its own safety stock (risk pooling works in reverse);
- facility costs rise, with more rent, staff and systems;
- inbound freight rises, because each site gets smaller, less efficient shipments.

So total logistics cost forms a **U-shape**: it falls at first, bottoms out, then climbs. A company competing on price sits near the bottom of the U with a few large warehouses. One competing on speed, such as same-day e-commerce or critical spare parts, deliberately builds past the cheapest point and pays for responsiveness.

<KeyIdea>

Network design decides roles, locations, capacities and allocations. The number of facilities is the main dial: more sites buy speed with inventory and facility cost, and the right setting depends on what customers value.

</KeyIdea>

<Q q="What happens to inventory, transport and facility costs as you add warehouses?">

Inventory rises because safety stock is split across more sites. Facility costs rise with more buildings. Outbound transport falls because customers are closer, but inbound transport rises because shipments to each site get smaller. Total cost is U-shaped, with a minimum somewhere in the middle.

</Q>

<Q q="Why might a company choose more warehouses than the cost-minimizing number?">

Because its customers pay for speed. Same-day delivery promises or critical spare parts justify the extra inventory and facility cost; being slower would lose more revenue than the network saves.

</Q>
