<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="What runs next on the line, and why the rule you pick changes the result." soon>

Planning decides how much to make this week. **Scheduling** decides exactly what runs on which line, in which order, starting at what time:

> 8:00–10:00 Shampoo A · 10:00–10:30 changeover · 10:30–13:00 Shampoo B · 13:00–14:00 Shampoo C

The order matters because jobs compete for the same machines and each one has a due date. Simple **dispatching rules** pick the next job, and each rule optimizes something different:

- **FIFO (first in, first out):** fair and simple, but blind to due dates and job length.
- **EDD (earliest due date):** run the most urgent job first. It minimizes the worst lateness.
- **SPT (shortest processing time):** run the quickest job first. It minimizes the average time jobs wait, but long jobs can sit for ages.

Real lines add complications. Changeover time often depends on the sequence: going from a light shampoo to a dark one is quick, while the reverse needs a full clean. Good schedules group similar products to cut changeovers. Then there are breakdowns, material shortages, shift patterns and maintenance windows.

That's why large plants use finite-capacity scheduling or **APS (advanced planning and scheduling)** software, which respects capacity and sequence rules and recalculates when something goes wrong, instead of assuming infinite capacity the way basic MRP does.

<KeyIdea>

There is no single best sequence, only the best one for your goal. EDD protects due dates, SPT cuts waiting time, and grouping similar products cuts changeovers.

</KeyIdea>

<SayIt>

Scheduling turns the production plan into an exact sequence of jobs on each resource. Dispatching rules give quick answers: EDD minimizes maximum lateness, SPT minimizes average flow time, and FIFO is simple but ignores both. Real plants also sequence to minimize sequence-dependent changeovers and respect maintenance and shifts, often with finite-capacity APS tools that reschedule when disruptions hit.

</SayIt>

<Q q="Which rule would you use to minimize late orders, and which to minimize average waiting time?">

Earliest due date minimizes the maximum lateness. Shortest processing time minimizes average flow time but can delay long jobs. In practice, plants often combine rules with changeover-aware sequencing.

</Q>

<Q q="Why does sequence matter so much in a paint or shampoo plant?">

Because changeover time depends on the order. Light to dark colours or fragrances need little cleaning, while the reverse needs a full washout. Sequencing in a sensible order can recover hours of capacity every week without new equipment.

</Q>
