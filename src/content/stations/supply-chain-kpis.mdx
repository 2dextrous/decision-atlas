<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="Service, cost and cash: one KPI from each, read together." next="How it works" nextMinutes={3}>

Supply chain performance is usually judged on three fronts at once: **service** to customers, **cost**, and **cash** tied up. The trouble is that they pull against each other: more stock improves service but costs cash, and fuller trucks cut cost but can slow delivery. That's why KPIs must be read together, never alone.

The ones that come up most:

| KPI | What it measures | Formula or meaning |
|---|---|---|
| OTIF | Orders delivered on time and in full | On-time-in-full orders ÷ total orders |
| Fill rate | Share of demand met from stock | Units shipped from stock ÷ units ordered |
| Perfect order | On time, complete, undamaged, correct documents | Product of the component rates |
| Inventory turns | How fast stock moves | Cost of goods sold ÷ average inventory |
| Days of inventory (DIO) | How long stock sits | 365 ÷ turns, or inventory ÷ daily COGS |
| Forecast accuracy and bias | Quality of the demand plan | WMAPE or MASE, plus mean error |
| Logistics cost | Cost efficiency | Logistics cost ÷ revenue, or cost per unit |
| Cash-to-cash cycle | Working capital tied up | DIO + DSO − DPO |
| OEE | Production effectiveness | Availability × performance × quality |

A company turning inventory 8 times a year holds about 46 days of stock (365 ÷ 8). Push turns to 12 and stock drops to about 30 days, freeing cash, but only if OTIF holds.

Frameworks like the **SCOR model** group measures into reliability, responsiveness, agility, cost and asset efficiency. A balanced set from each group stops a team from improving one number by damaging another, such as cutting inventory until service collapses.

<KeyIdea>

Read KPIs as a set: service, cost and cash. Any single KPI can be "improved" by quietly hurting another.

</KeyIdea>

### From KPIs to return on capital

Senior leaders judge the business on return on capital employed (ROCE): operating profit divided by the capital tied up. Supply chain performance moves both parts:

- **Profit:** cost of goods sold, logistics cost, obsolescence, expediting, and revenue lost to stockouts.
- **Capital:** inventory, receivables minus payables, and fixed assets such as plants, warehouses and trucks.

<Example title="a modest improvement">

Revenue ₹500 crore, operating profit ₹50 crore, capital employed ₹250 crore: ROCE is 20%.

- Logistics savings of ₹2 crore lift profit to ₹52 crore.
- Removing ₹10 crore of excess inventory cuts capital to ₹240 crore, and avoids about ₹1 crore a year of storage and obsolescence cost.
- ROCE becomes 53 / 240 ≈ 22.1%.

A two-point rise in ROCE from supply chain work alone, without any growth in sales.

</Example>

### Define KPIs precisely

The same name can hide different calculations. A KPI definition should state:

- **Formula and units:** OTIF by order, line or unit?
- **Reference point:** on time to the customer's requested date or the promised date? At dispatch or at delivery?
- **Scope:** which customers, products, sites and channels.
- **Data source** and **timing:** where the data comes from, how often it is calculated, and when.
- **Owner and target.**

Without this, two sites report the same KPI with different meanings, and comparisons mislead.

### Leading and lagging indicators

**Lagging** indicators (OTIF, inventory turns, cost per unit) report results. **Leading** indicators warn of trouble earlier: forecast bias, projected stock below safety stock, supplier acknowledgement delays, backlog of open orders. A good dashboard pairs each important result with the signals that predict it.

<KeyIdea>

Supply chain KPIs connect to return on capital through profit and capital employed. Define every KPI precisely (formula, reference point, scope, source, owner), and pair lagging results with leading signals.

</KeyIdea>

### Balance, not a single number

Kaplan and Norton's balanced scorecard (1992) argued that no single measure can guide an organization; financial results must be read with customer, process and learning measures. For supply chains, the balance is service, cost, cash and, increasingly, resilience and sustainability. SCOR organizes its top-level metrics similarly, across reliability, responsiveness, agility, cost and asset efficiency.

### Goodhart's law

"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." A forecast accuracy target invites sandbagging; an inventory target at year end invites delayed receipts; an OTIF target measured against promised dates invites late promises. Designing KPIs means anticipating how people will respond to them and pairing measures so that gaming one shows up in another.

### Functional KPIs that fight each other

- **Purchase price variance** rewards buyers for big discounted buys that raise inventory and obsolescence.
- **Plant efficiency** rewards long runs that build stock and delay other products.
- **Transport cost per kilometre** rewards cheap carriers who deliver late.
- **Sales revenue** at quarter end rewards loading the channel with stock.

Each looks sensible locally. Shared end-to-end measures, such as total landed cost, OTIF to the customer and cash conversion, align the functions.

### Benchmarking with care

External benchmarks are useful only when definitions, business models and product mixes are comparable. Inventory turns differ widely between grocery and machinery; OTIF differs by how it is measured. Benchmarks are best used to ask questions ("why are our turns lower than similar companies'?"), not to set targets mechanically.

### Incentives

Bonuses tied to a single supply chain KPI tend to distort decisions. Combinations of team-level and end-to-end measures, with a few individual measures that the person can actually influence, work better, as does reviewing how results were achieved, not just whether targets were hit.

<Trap>

A dashboard with fifty KPIs is a report, not a management tool. Choose a handful of balanced, well-defined measures that drive decisions, and keep the rest for diagnosis.

</Trap>

<KeyIdea>

Balance service, cost, cash, resilience and sustainability; expect Goodhart's law and pair measures to expose gaming; replace conflicting functional KPIs with end-to-end ones; benchmark with care; and design incentives around shared results.

</KeyIdea>

<Q q="Inventory turns rose from 8 to 12. Is that good?">

Days of inventory fell from about 46 to about 30, freeing cash. But it's only good if service held: check OTIF, fill rate and expediting costs. If service dropped or emergency freight rose, the improvement may be costing more than it saves.

</Q>

<Q q="Which three KPIs would you show a CEO for the supply chain?">

One for each front: OTIF for service, total supply chain cost as a share of revenue for cost, and the cash conversion cycle or days of inventory for cash. Together they show whether service is improving without hurting cost or working capital.

</Q>
