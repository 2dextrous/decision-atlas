<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="Six steps from a demand map to a recommended network." soon>

A **greenfield** study designs the network as if nothing existed yet: no plants or warehouses you must keep. A **brownfield** study starts from today's network and asks what to keep, close, move or add. Greenfield answers are useful even for companies with networks, because they show the ideal shape and how far the current one is from it.

A typical study for an FMCG company expanding across India runs like this:

1. **Map the demand.** Where are customers, and how much does each region buy, say North 2.5 lakh units, West 3.5 lakh, South 2.5 lakh, East 1.5 lakh? Look at the future too: design for the demand in five years, not today's.
2. **Build scenarios.** Low, base and high demand, because a network has to work across a range of futures.
3. **List candidate sites.** Mumbai, Pune, Ahmedabad, Delhi, Hyderabad, Bengaluru, Kolkata, Chennai, filtered by land, labour, roads and rail, power, taxes and incentives.
4. **Collect costs.** Fixed cost per site, handling cost, freight rates per lane, inventory carrying cost, and service targets such as delivery within 48 hours.
5. **Optimize.** A mixed-integer model chooses which candidates to open and how product flows, at minimum total cost within service limits.
6. **Stress-test.** Rerun with fuel prices up, a site closed or demand shifted. The best design stays good across scenarios, not just the base case.

The output isn't just a map. It is a recommended network with the cost, service and risk of each alternative, which is what leadership needs to commit years of investment.

<Q q="What is the difference between greenfield and brownfield design?">

Greenfield assumes no existing facilities and finds the ideal network. Brownfield starts from current sites, contracts and investments and decides what to keep, close, relocate or add, so switching costs and timing matter. Companies often run both: greenfield to see the ideal, brownfield to plan realistic steps towards it.

</Q>

<Q q="Why design for scenarios rather than a single forecast?">

Because the network lasts for years and demand will differ from any single forecast. A design that's optimal only in the base case can be badly undersized or wasteful in other futures. Testing across scenarios finds designs that are good enough everywhere.

</Q>
