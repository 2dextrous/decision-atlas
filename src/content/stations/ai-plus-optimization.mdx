<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="Prediction tells you what's coming; optimization tells you what to do about it." next="How it works" nextMinutes={3}>

AI and optimization solve two different halves of a supply chain decision.

**AI and machine learning predict and understand.** "Next week's demand will be about 120,000 units, probably between 110,000 and 135,000." "This supplier is likely to deliver late." "This contract has a price-escalation clause."

**Optimization decides under constraints.** Given that demand, which plants should make how much, which warehouses should receive it, and which trucks should move it, at the lowest cost while meeting 95% service, staying within capacity and respecting minimum batches?

Each one alone falls short:

- A forecast with no optimization leaves people to work out the plan by hand, often badly.
- An optimization fed a single wrong number produces an excellent plan for the wrong future.

Together they make a decision pipeline: **predict → optimize → act**. Better still, the optimization uses the *range* of predictions, not just the middle number, so the plan holds up if demand comes in at 135,000 rather than 120,000. That's optimization under uncertainty, using scenarios or probabilistic forecasts.

Agents add a third layer: they notice the trigger ("demand jumped 15%"), gather the data, call the forecasting and optimization tools, explain the recommendation in plain language, and route it for approval.

A compact way to remember it: **AI predicts and understands, optimization decides within constraints, agents orchestrate and explain, and people approve what matters.**

<KeyIdea>

Prediction without optimization leaves the decision unmade; optimization without good predictions solves the wrong problem. Feed the range of predictions into optimization, and let agents connect the steps.

</KeyIdea>

### Point forecast or range?

<Example title="how much to produce for next month">

Demand scenarios: 110,000 units (25% likely), 120,000 (50%) and 135,000 (25%). Each unit sold earns ₹40. Each unit left over costs ₹15 (holding and markdowns). Each unit short costs the lost ₹40 plus a ₹10 penalty.

Expected profit for three production plans:

<div class="table-wrap">

| Produce | Expected profit |
|---|---|
| 110,000 | ₹42.9 lakh |
| 120,000 (the point forecast) | ₹46.25 lakh |
| 135,000 | ₹46.44 lakh |

</div>

Planning on the point forecast of 120,000 is not the best choice. Because a shortage costs more than a leftover, the best plan leans towards the high scenario. In newsvendor terms the critical ratio is (40 + 10) / (40 + 10 + 15) ≈ 0.77, well above the median.

</Example>

The difference here is modest, but it grows with the asymmetry of costs and the width of the range, and in network or capacity decisions it can decide whether a plan survives a bad month.

### The predict-optimize-act pipeline

1. **Predict:** forecasts with ranges (quantiles or scenarios), lead-time distributions, supplier risks.
2. **Optimize:** a model that uses those ranges, through scenarios (stochastic programming), protection against bad cases (robust optimization), or simple quantile rules where they fit.
3. **Act:** release orders and plans, with explanations: which constraints bind, what extra capacity would be worth (shadow prices), and how sensitive the plan is.
4. **Learn:** compare outcomes with predictions and plans, and update both models.

<KeyIdea>

Optimizing on a single forecast throws away what you know about uncertainty. Feed ranges or scenarios into the optimization, so the plan reflects the costs of being over and under, then act and learn from the results.

</KeyIdea>

### Train predictions for the decision

Standard practice trains forecasts to minimize prediction error, then feeds them to an optimizer. But the decision cares only about errors that change the decision. **Smart "predict, then optimize"** (Elmachtoub and Grigas, 2022) trains the prediction model to minimize the cost of the resulting decisions instead, which can give better decisions even when the forecasts look slightly less accurate by conventional measures.

### Prescriptive analytics

Bertsimas and Kallus (2020) showed how to go from data and machine learning directly to decisions: use methods such as nearest neighbours or tree ensembles to find past situations similar to today's, and optimize against the outcomes observed in those situations. This uses rich data (weather, promotions, events) without having to forecast every quantity separately.

### Machine learning inside solvers

Learning also helps optimization itself: predicting good starting solutions, choosing which variables to branch on, tuning solver settings, or learning fast heuristics for problems solved repeatedly, such as daily routing. The optimizer still guarantees feasibility; learning makes it faster.

### Language models and optimization

Language models can help translate a planner's description into a draft optimization model, explain results in plain language, and answer "why" questions about a plan using its shadow prices and binding constraints. Formulations they produce still need expert checking: a constraint in the wrong direction gives a confident, wrong plan.

### Earning planners' trust

- **Explain:** show binding constraints, trade-offs and what changes the answer.
- **Compare:** run the new approach alongside the current method and show results over time.
- **Allow overrides,** recorded with reasons, and learn from them.
- **Start where the value is clear** and the decision repeats often, then expand.

<Trap>

Feeding a biased forecast into an excellent optimizer produces an excellent plan for the wrong future, with false confidence attached. Check forecast bias and range calibration before trusting optimized plans.

</Trap>

<KeyIdea>

Train predictions for decision quality, consider prescriptive methods that go from data to decisions, use learning to speed up solvers, let language models draft and explain but not certify models, and build planners' trust through explanation, comparison and recorded overrides.

</KeyIdea>

<Q q="Will AI replace optimization in supply chains?">

No, they do different jobs. AI is strong at prediction, pattern recognition and understanding unstructured information; optimization is built to find the best feasible decision under hard constraints, with guarantees. The strongest systems use AI to produce inputs and orchestrate, and optimization to decide.

</Q>

<Q q="Why feed a forecast range rather than a single number into optimization?">

Because a plan optimized for one number can fail when reality differs. Using scenarios or quantiles lets the optimization choose plans that hold up across likely outcomes, for example keeping some flexible capacity, at a small cost in the base case.

</Q>
