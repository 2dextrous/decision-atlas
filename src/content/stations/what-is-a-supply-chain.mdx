<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="Stages, three flows, and the line between planning ahead and reacting to orders." next="How it works" nextMinutes={3}>

Think about a bottle of shampoo in a kirana store. Before it got there, a chemical supplier made the surfactants, a packaging supplier moulded the bottle, a factory filled and packed it, a warehouse stored it, a distributor delivered it, and the store put it on the shelf. All of those **stages**, plus the customer who buys it, form a supply chain.

Three things flow through it:

- **Product** flows mostly downstream, from suppliers to customers, and some flows back as returns.
- **Information** flows both ways: orders and sales data upstream, delivery plans and invoices downstream.
- **Money** flows upstream as each stage pays the one before it.

Supply chain management is coordinating all three so the customer gets the product when and where they want it, at the lowest total cost. The total matters, because a choice that saves money in one stage, such as huge factory batches, can cost more elsewhere, in warehouse space and stale stock.

One useful idea is the **push–pull boundary**:

- **Push** steps run ahead of demand, on forecasts: the factory makes shampoo before anyone orders it.
- **Pull** steps run in response to an actual order: a custom laptop is assembled only after you click "buy".

Where the boundary sits shapes everything: inventory is held just before it. Shampoo is pushed almost all the way to the shelf; a made-to-order sofa is pulled from much earlier.

<KeyIdea>

A supply chain is every stage involved in meeting a customer's need, linked by flows of product, information and money. Manage it for the total, not stage by stage.

</KeyIdea>

### Four cycles

A supply chain can be read as a series of cycles, each linking two stages and each starting with an order:

<div class="table-wrap">

| Cycle | Between | Triggered by |
|---|---|---|
| Customer order | Customer and retailer | A customer buying or ordering |
| Replenishment | Retailer and distributor | The retailer's stock running down |
| Manufacturing | Distributor and manufacturer | Replenishment orders or a forecast |
| Procurement | Manufacturer and suppliers | The production plan |

</div>

Each cycle has its own steps (the buyer orders, the supplier fulfils and delivers, the buyer receives and pays) and its own people and systems. Problems often appear at the hand-offs between cycles.

### Three kinds of decision

- **Design (years):** where plants and warehouses go, what to make or buy, which modes and partners to use.
- **Planning (months to a year):** how much to produce where, which markets each site serves, inventory targets, promotions.
- **Operation (days to weeks):** which order ships today, from where, on which truck; which line runs which product.

Each level sets the limits for the next one down, as the planning hierarchy stop explains.

<Example title="a shampoo's 72 days">

Typical times between a surfactant being made and the shampoo being sold:

<div class="table-wrap">

| Stage | Days |
|---|---|
| Transit, chemical supplier to plant | 5 |
| Raw material stock at the plant | 15 |
| Production and packing | 2 |
| Finished goods at the plant | 10 |
| Transit to the distribution centre | 3 |
| Stock at the distribution centre | 20 |
| At the distributor | 7 |
| On the store shelf | 10 |
| **Total** | **72** |

</div>

Only about 2 of those 72 days change the product; the rest is moving and waiting. Most of a supply chain's inventory, and much of its cost, sits in that waiting, which is why end-to-end lead time is such a powerful measure.

</Example>

<KeyIdea>

A supply chain runs as linked cycles (order, replenishment, manufacturing, procurement) and is managed through design, planning and operational decisions. Tracing a product end to end shows how much of the lead time is waiting rather than work.

</KeyIdea>

### Where the idea came from

Logistics, purchasing and production were long managed as separate functions. The term "supply chain management" was popularized in the early 1980s by consultants Keith Oliver and Michael Webber, who argued that the flow from suppliers to customers should be managed as one system, with inventory and service decided across functions rather than within each.

### The SCOR model

The Supply Chain Operations Reference model, maintained by ASCM, gives a standard vocabulary for supply chain processes and metrics. The classic version organized processes as Plan, Source, Make, Deliver, Return and Enable; the newer SCOR Digital Standard uses Orchestrate, Plan, Order, Source, Transform, Fulfill and Return. Companies use it to map their processes, compare performance on common metrics and find gaps.

### Local versus global

Decisions that look good for one stage often hurt the whole:

- Purchasing buys a year's supply to get a discount; the warehouse fills up and part of it becomes obsolete.
- The plant runs long batches to cut changeover costs; distribution centres hold months of stock.
- Sales books a big end-of-quarter order with a special discount; production pays overtime and next quarter's orders fall.

Managing the supply chain means making these trade-offs visible and judging them on total cost and service, which usually requires shared measures and incentives across functions.

### Chains are really networks

Real supply chains are networks: many suppliers at each tier, several plants, many channels and customers, with service providers (logistics companies, banks, IT providers) supporting the flows. Decisions at one point ripple through the network, which is why visibility and coordination matter as much as any single function's efficiency.

### New objectives

Cost and service were the classic goals. Resilience (surviving disruptions) and sustainability (emissions, social impact, circularity) have joined them, and many supply chain decisions now trade off all four.

<Trap>

Treating supply chain management as just logistics, or just purchasing, misses its point: coordinating decisions across all stages and functions so the whole performs well, even when one part has to accept a higher local cost.

</Trap>

<KeyIdea>

Supply chain management grew from the idea of managing the flow as one system. SCOR gives a common process language, local optimizations must be judged on total effect, real chains are networks, and resilience and sustainability now sit beside cost and service.

</KeyIdea>

<Q q="What is the push–pull boundary, and where is it for a shampoo versus a custom laptop?">

It's the point where the supply chain switches from forecast-driven to order-driven work. For shampoo it's at the store shelf: everything up to there is made and moved on forecasts. For a made-to-order laptop it's at assembly: components are pushed to the factory, but final assembly happens only after the order.

</Q>

<Q q="Why optimize the whole supply chain rather than each stage?">

Because stages trade off against each other. A factory minimizing its own cost might run huge batches, which raises warehouse and obsolescence costs downstream. Only a total view finds the lowest overall cost at the service customers need.

</Q>
