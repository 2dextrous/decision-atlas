<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="Two questions, how much it matters and how risky it is to get, give four strategies." soon>

Not everything a company buys deserves the same treatment. Peter Kraljic's 1983 matrix sorts purchases on two questions:

- **Profit impact:** how much money flows through this category, and how much it affects product cost and quality?
- **Supply risk:** how hard is it to get: few suppliers, scarce materials, long lead times, specialist know-how?

That gives four boxes, each with its own strategy:

| | Low supply risk | High supply risk |
|---|---|---|
| **High profit impact** | **Leverage** (standard packaging, common chemicals): many suppliers, so compete them through tenders and auctions, consolidate volume and negotiate hard | **Strategic** (a key active ingredient, an advanced chip): build long-term partnerships, plan jointly, share forecasts and manage risk together |
| **Low profit impact** | **Non-critical** (office supplies, cleaning materials): automate buying with catalogues and purchasing cards and spend as little effort as possible | **Bottleneck** (a special spare part from one source): secure supply with safety stock, backup suppliers or redesign to remove the dependency |

The common mistake is using one approach everywhere: hammering a strategic supplier on price can damage a relationship you depend on, while building partnerships for office paper wastes time. Categories also move between boxes, for example when a single-source supplier gets a competitor, or when a commodity suddenly becomes scarce.

<KeyIdea>

Match the buying strategy to the category: compete leverage items, partner on strategic ones, secure bottlenecks and automate the non-critical.

</KeyIdea>

<SayIt>

The Kraljic matrix classifies purchases by profit impact and supply risk. Leverage items are high-impact, low-risk, so we use competition and volume. Strategic items are high-impact, high-risk, so we build partnerships and joint planning. Bottleneck items are low-impact but risky, so we secure supply with stock, backups or redesign. Non-critical items get automated, low-effort buying. Each category gets the strategy that fits, and the classification is reviewed as markets change.

</SayIt>

<Q q="A component costs little but has only one qualified supplier. Which quadrant, and what would you do?">

Bottleneck. The priority is continuity, not price: hold safety stock, qualify a second supplier, sign longer agreements to secure capacity, or redesign the product to use a standard part.

</Q>

<Q q="Why not run reverse auctions for every category?">

Auctions work for leverage items with many capable suppliers and clear specifications. For strategic items they can damage relationships, cut supplier investment and raise risk; for bottleneck items there may be no competition to exploit.

</Q>
