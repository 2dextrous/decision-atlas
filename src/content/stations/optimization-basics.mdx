<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="Decide, aim, respect the limits: the three parts of every optimization model." next="How it works" nextMinutes={3}>

Every optimization model has three parts:

- **Decision variables:** what you're choosing, such as how much of each product to make or which warehouse to open.
- **Objective:** what you're maximizing or minimizing, such as profit or total cost.
- **Constraints:** the limits you must respect, such as machine hours, labour, demand and capacity.

Here's a small **linear program (LP)**. A plant makes products A and B, earning ₹50 and ₹40 per unit. A needs 2 machine hours and 1 labour hour; B needs 1 machine hour and 2 labour hours. There are 100 machine hours and 80 labour hours.

$$
\max\ 50x_1 + 40x_2 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad 2x_1 + x_2 \le 100,\quad x_1 + 2x_2 \le 80,\quad x_1, x_2 \ge 0
$$

The best plan uses both resources fully: make **40 of A and 20 of B**, for a profit of **₹2,800**. Making only A would give 50 units and ₹2,500; only B, 40 units and ₹1,600. The optimum sits at a corner where both constraints meet, which is how LP solutions always behave.

It's called *linear* because every term is a number times a variable, with no variables multiplied together. That makes LPs fast to solve, even with millions of variables.

Many supply chain decisions are yes-or-no: open this warehouse or not, use this supplier or not, run this line or not. Those need **integer** (often 0/1) variables, which makes the model a **mixed-integer linear program (MILP)**. MILPs are much harder to solve, so they use specialist **solvers** such as Gurobi, CPLEX, or open-source ones like HiGHS and CBC. Small models even run in Excel Solver.

<KeyIdea>

Optimization means choosing decision variables to maximize or minimize an objective within constraints. LP handles quantities; MILP adds the yes-or-no decisions that network and sourcing problems need.

</KeyIdea>

### Solving the LP by its corners

An LP's best solution is always at a corner of the feasible region (or along an edge between two equally good corners). For the gist's problem, the corners are:

<div class="table-wrap">

| Corner | Product A | Product B | Profit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Make nothing | 0 | 0 | ₹0 |
| Machine hours limit, only A | 50 | 0 | ₹2,500 |
| Both limits binding | 40 | 20 | **₹2,800** |
| Labour hours limit, only B | 0 | 40 | ₹1,600 |

</div>

Solvers don't list corners (there can be billions); the simplex method walks from corner to better corner until no neighbour improves.

### Shadow prices: what each resource is worth

At the optimum both resources are fully used. How much would one more hour of each add?

<Example title="valuing machine and labour hours">

Write $y_1$ for the value of a machine hour and $y_2$ for a labour hour. At the optimum, each product's profit equals the value of the resources it uses:

- Product A: $2y_1 + y_2 = 50$
- Product B: $y_1 + 2y_2 = 40$

Solving gives $y_1$ = ₹20 and $y_2$ = ₹10. One extra machine hour adds ₹20 of profit; one extra labour hour adds ₹10. Check: 100 × ₹20 + 80 × ₹10 = ₹2,800, exactly the optimal profit.

</Example>

So if overtime on the machine costs less than ₹20 an hour, it pays; labour overtime pays only below ₹10 an hour. Shadow prices hold within a range: beyond some point, another constraint starts to bind and the values change. Solvers report these ranges in a **sensitivity report**.

### Yes-or-no decisions with binary variables

Real supply chain decisions need logic that plain LPs can't express. Binary variables ($y$ = 0 or 1) handle it:

- **Fixed cost:** a warehouse with fixed cost $f$ and capacity $K$: add $f\,y$ to the cost and require flow $x \le K\,y$, so a closed site carries nothing.
- **Minimum batch:** if a product is made at all, make at least $m$: $m\,y \le x \le M\,y$.
- **Either-or:** use at most two of five suppliers: $y_1 + \dots + y_5 \le 2$.

<KeyIdea>

LP optima sit at corners, shadow prices value each scarce resource, and binary variables bring in fixed costs, minimum batches and either-or rules, turning LPs into the MILPs that network and sourcing decisions need.

</KeyIdea>

### How MILP solvers work

- **Branch and bound:** solve the LP version (ignoring integrality). If a binary variable comes out at 0.6, branch into two subproblems, one with it fixed at 0 and one at 1, and keep the best integer solution found as a benchmark to prune branches that can't beat it.
- **Cutting planes:** add constraints that cut off fractional solutions without removing any integer ones, tightening the LP bound.
- **Heuristics:** quickly find good integer solutions to prune more of the tree.

Modern solvers combine all three (branch and cut) and solve problems that were out of reach a few decades ago.

### Formulation strength

Two formulations of the same problem can solve at very different speeds. In $x \le M\,y$, choosing $M$ far larger than needed lets the LP relaxation set $y$ to a tiny fraction, giving weak bounds and huge search trees. Use the smallest valid $M$ (a site's true capacity, or total demand), and prefer formulations whose LP relaxation is close to the integer problem.

### The optimality gap

Solvers report the best solution found and a bound on how good the best possible solution could be. The **gap** between them measures how far from optimal the current answer might be. Stopping at 1% often saves hours, and for decisions based on uncertain forecasts, a 1% gap is usually irrelevant.

### Tools

Models are written in modelling languages or libraries (such as Pyomo, PuLP or Google OR-Tools in Python, or AMPL and GAMS), which keep data separate from the model and connect to commercial or open-source solvers. Excel Solver suits small teaching models.

### When a model misbehaves

- **Infeasible:** no solution meets all constraints. Look for conflicting data, such as demand above total capacity, or a constraint written in the wrong direction. Solvers can identify a minimal conflicting set.
- **Unbounded:** profit can grow forever, usually a missing constraint or a sign error.
- **Strange answers:** check units, data scaling and whether the objective really reflects the decision.

### Beyond linear

Some costs are not linear: safety stock grows with a square root, and quantity discounts jump. Common techniques approximate them piecewise with linear segments, or use specialized nonlinear solvers. Uncertainty can be handled with scenarios (stochastic programming) or by protecting against worst cases within a range (robust optimization).

<Trap>

An optimal solution is optimal only for the model and data you gave it. Check the inputs, test sensitivity to the uncertain ones, and look for answers that seem too good, which usually point to a missing constraint.

</Trap>

<KeyIdea>

MILP solvers use branch and cut; tight formulations with small big-M values solve faster; a small optimality gap is usually good enough; and infeasible, unbounded or surprising results almost always trace back to data or formulation errors.

</KeyIdea>

<Q q="Maximize 50x₁ + 40x₂ with 2x₁ + x₂ ≤ 100 and x₁ + 2x₂ ≤ 80. What is the optimum?">

Check the corners of the feasible region: (50, 0) gives 2,500, (0, 40) gives 1,600, and where both constraints meet, x₁ = 40 and x₂ = 20, it gives 2,800. So the optimum is 40 of A and 20 of B, profit 2,800.

</Q>

<Q q="When do you need a MILP instead of an LP?">

When decisions are discrete: open or close a site, choose a supplier, run a line or not, fixed costs that only apply if something is used, or minimum batch sizes. Those need integer or binary variables. Pure quantity decisions with linear costs stay as an LP.

</Q>
