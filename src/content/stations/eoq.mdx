<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="Two opposing costs, one sweet spot, and why being roughly right is enough." soon>

Every time you place an order, it costs something fixed: paperwork, calls, a delivery run, receiving and checking. Every unit you keep in stock costs something too: cash tied up, storage, spoilage. Order rarely in big batches and you save on ordering but pay to hold a mountain of stock. Order often in small batches and you hold little but pay the ordering cost again and again.

The **economic order quantity (EOQ)** is the order size where the total of those two costs is lowest:

$$
EOQ = \sqrt{\frac{2DS}{H}}
$$

where $D$ is annual demand, $S$ the cost per order and $H$ the cost of holding one unit for a year.

Take a café in Pune that uses 1,200 kg of coffee beans a year. Each order costs about ₹500 to place, and holding a kilogram for a year costs about ₹200. Then $EOQ = \sqrt{2 \times 1200 \times 500 / 200} = \sqrt{6000} \approx 77.5$ kg, about 15 orders a year. At that size the yearly ordering cost and holding cost come out equal, around ₹7,750 each.

The useful surprise is how forgiving it is. If the café orders 100 kg instead (29% more than the EOQ), its total cost rises by only about 3%. So you can round EOQ to a pack size, a truck load or a supplier's minimum without losing much.

<KeyIdea>

EOQ answers *how much* to order, not *when*. And because the cost curve is flat near the bottom, a practical quantity close to the EOQ is good enough.

</KeyIdea>

<SayIt>

EOQ balances two opposing costs: ordering often costs more in fixed ordering costs, ordering big costs more to hold. The optimum is the square root of 2DS over H, where the two costs are equal. I treat it as a starting point. The cost curve is flat near the optimum, so I'd round to pack sizes, truck loads or supplier minimums, and check quantity discounts separately.

</SayIt>

<Q q="What happens to the EOQ if annual demand quadruples?">

It doubles, because EOQ grows with the square root of demand. Order frequency doubles too. That is why fast-moving items are ordered more often but not in proportionally bigger batches.

</Q>

<Q q="Why does it matter that the total cost curve is flat near the EOQ?">

Because small deviations barely hurt. In the café example, ordering 29% more than the EOQ raises the total cost by about 3%. You can round to practical lot sizes, and errors in estimating the ordering or holding cost don't do much damage.

</Q>

<Q q="What does EOQ assume, and when does it break?">

Steady demand, a fixed cost per order, a constant unit price and the whole batch arriving at once. It needs adjusting for quantity discounts (where a bigger order can be cheaper overall), for production that builds stock gradually, and for very uncertain or seasonal demand.

</Q>
