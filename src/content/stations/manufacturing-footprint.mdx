<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="Where to make things, and what job each plant should do." next="How it works" nextMinutes={3}>

A company's **manufacturing footprint** is its set of plants: where they are, how big they are, and what each one makes. Footprint decisions last decades, so they rest on more than today's wage rates:

- **Costs:** labour, land, power, taxes and incentives, and freight to markets.
- **Markets:** being near customers cuts lead time and freight, and sometimes local production is needed to sell at all.
- **Skills and suppliers:** a cluster of trained workers and component makers, like auto parts around Pune or Chennai.
- **Infrastructure and risk:** ports, roads, reliable power, political stability, exchange rates and tariffs.

Kasra Ferdows argued that plants should also have a clear **strategic role**, and that the best ones grow into bigger roles over time. He described six:

| Role | What the plant is for |
|---|---|
| Offshore | Makes cheaply to a specification set elsewhere; little local know-how |
| Source | Low-cost production, but with authority over its own processes and suppliers |
| Server | Supplies a specific regional market, often to avoid tariffs or freight |
| Contributor | Serves its region and also develops products and processes |
| Outpost | Placed mainly to gather knowledge: access to skills, suppliers or competitors |
| Lead | Creates new products and processes for the whole company |

The two views come together in practice. A plant set up as a low-cost offshore site can, with investment in skills, become a source or even a lead plant. One that stays offshore is easy to replace when costs rise.

<KeyIdea>

Choose plant locations on total landed cost, market access, skills and risk, not wages alone, and give each plant a clear strategic role that can grow over time.

</KeyIdea>

<Level n={2} title="How it works" minutes={3} teaser="Comparing locations on total landed cost, a worked example where higher wages still win, and how plants move up the role ladder." next="Deep dive" nextMinutes={3}>

### Total landed cost, not wages

A footprint comparison adds up every cost of getting a unit to the customer from each candidate location:

- materials, labour, overhead and energy at the plant,
- freight and insurance to the market,
- duties and taxes at the border,
- the carrying cost of stock in a longer pipeline,
- and, where it matters, an allowance for risk: currency swings, disruption, quality problems.

<Example title="a product for Indian customers">

Two options: a plant in India, or a lower-wage plant in a hypothetical Country X, shipping by sea. Costs per unit:

<div class="table-wrap">

| | India | Country X |
|---|---|---|
| Materials | ₹300 | ₹300 |
| Labour | ₹60 | ₹40 |
| Overhead and energy | ₹40 | ₹35 |
| Freight to customers | ₹15 | ₹45 |
| Import duty (a hypothetical 10% of the ₹375 factory price) | none | ₹37.5 |
| Pipeline stock: 6 extra weeks at a 25% yearly holding rate | none | ₹12.1 |
| **Landed cost** | **₹415** | **₹469.6** |

</div>

Country X saves ₹20 of labour and loses far more on freight, duty and pipeline stock. Labour is often a small share of total cost, so wage gaps alone rarely decide a location.

</Example>

### What else goes into the decision

- **Market access:** some markets require or reward local production through tariffs, local-content rules or incentives such as India's production-linked incentive schemes.
- **Speed:** a nearby plant can respond in days, not weeks, which matters for short-lifecycle or fast-changing products.
- **Skills and supplier base:** clusters of trained people and component makers lower cost and risk over time.
- **Risk:** concentration in one country, currency exposure, political and natural hazards.

### Growing a plant's role

In Ferdows's framework, plants can move up from offshore and source roles to contributor and lead roles as they build skills and take on development work. Moving up needs deliberate investment: engineering staff, authority over processes and suppliers, and responsibility for products, not just volume.

<KeyIdea>

Compare locations on total landed cost (materials, labour, overhead, freight, duty and pipeline stock), then weigh market access, speed, skills and risk. Labour is usually a small share, so wages alone rarely decide.

</KeyIdea>

<Level n={3} title="Deep dive" minutes={3} teaser="Focused factories, regional versus global footprints, flexible capacity as a hedge, and the hidden costs of distance.">

### Focus and scale

Skinner's "focused factory" (1974) argued that a plant doing a limited set of things, for one kind of market, outperforms one trying to do everything. Footprints therefore often specialize plants: one by product family, another by process technology, another by market. The cost is reduced flexibility: if one product family's demand collapses, its focused plant has nothing else to make.

### Three footprint patterns

- **Centralized:** one or a few large plants serve the world. Maximum scale and focus, long supply lines and high exposure to a single site.
- **Regional ("in the region, for the region"):** plants in each major market. Shorter lead times, lower tariffs and freight, local responsiveness; less scale.
- **Hub and spoke:** central plants make core components, regional plants do final assembly or customization, which combines scale with postponement.

Trade tensions, tariffs and pandemic disruptions pushed many companies towards regional footprints and "China plus one" strategies, adding capacity in other countries, including India, to reduce concentration risk.

### Capacity as an option

Demand, costs and trade rules will change during a plant's life, so flexibility has value:

- **Flexible plants** that can make several product families let the footprint absorb shifts in mix.
- **Modular capacity,** added in steps as demand appears, avoids betting everything on one forecast.
- **Partners and contract manufacturers** provide capacity without capital, at the cost of control and margin.

Valuing these options properly means modelling scenarios, not single forecasts.

### Hidden costs of distance

Studies of offshoring decisions often found costs that were missed at the start: travel and supervision, quality problems found late, slower engineering changes, larger safety stocks for long and variable lead times, intellectual-property risk, and the management attention needed to coordinate across time zones. Including these in the comparison, even roughly, changes many decisions.

<Trap>

Moving production for a lower wage rate without counting freight, duties, pipeline stock, quality and coordination costs can raise total cost while appearing to cut it. Insist on a total landed cost comparison with risk included.

</Trap>

<KeyIdea>

Focused plants perform better but flex less; footprints range from centralized to regional to hub and spoke. Treat flexible and modular capacity as options worth paying for, and count the hidden costs of distance before moving production.

</KeyIdea>

<Q q="Why might a company build a plant in a high-cost country?">

To be close to a key market, avoid tariffs or long lead times, access skills or suppliers, or develop products alongside customers. Ferdows calls these server, outpost or lead roles. Total landed cost and strategic value can outweigh higher wages.

</Q>

<Q q="What is the risk of treating every plant as a low-cost offshore site?">

Those plants hold little know-how, so they're easy to replace and contribute nothing to improvement or new products. When costs shift, the company has to move again. Plants with deeper roles create advantages that are harder to copy.

</Q>
