<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="Keep what makes you different, outsource what others do better, and book partner capacity early." soon>

Every company decides what to make itself and what to buy. The usual reasons to **make** something in-house are that it's core to your advantage (a secret formula, a key technology), that quality or intellectual property needs tight control, or that you have spare capacity. The usual reasons to **buy** are that a specialist does it cheaper or better, that you'd rather not tie up capital in plants, or that demand is too uncertain to justify your own capacity.

In FMCG this often means **contract manufacturing**: a co-packer or co-manufacturer makes or packs products under your brand. A shampoo company might make its core packs in its own plants and use co-packers for:

- seasonal peaks and promotions, when its own plants are full,
- new products not yet proven enough to justify a new line,
- regional supply close to a market,
- special formats such as sachets or gift packs.

Two decisions follow. **Allocation:** how much volume each co-packer gets, weighing their price, capacity, quality and minimum volumes against your own plants. **Capacity reservation:** good co-packers are busy before festive seasons, so companies often book capacity months ahead, paying a reservation or commitment fee in exchange for guaranteed slots.

The risks of outsourcing are real: less control over quality and timing, leaked know-how, weaker bargaining power if you depend on one partner, and lost internal skills.

<KeyIdea>

Make what gives you an advantage or needs tight control; buy what partners do better or cheaper. When you rely on co-packers, plan their volume in S&OP and book their capacity before peak season.

</KeyIdea>

<SayIt>

Make-or-buy depends on strategic importance, cost and capability, capacity and risk. I'd keep core, differentiating or IP-sensitive production in-house and outsource where partners are cheaper, better or more flexible. With co-manufacturers, volume allocation and capacity reservation become planning decisions: allocate by cost, capacity and quality within S&OP, and book capacity ahead of peaks, while managing quality, dependency and know-how risks.

<Q q="When would you use a co-packer instead of building a new line?">

When demand is seasonal or uncertain, such as a new product or a festive peak, when you need capacity faster than you can build it, when a partner offers a special format or a location near the market, or when the capital is better spent elsewhere.

</Q>

<Q q="What are the risks of relying heavily on contract manufacturers?">

Quality and delivery are less under your control, know-how can leak to competitors, you lose internal skills, and you become dependent. If capacity is tight industry-wide, partners may favour bigger clients. Contracts, audits, dual partners and capacity reservations reduce these risks.

</Q>
