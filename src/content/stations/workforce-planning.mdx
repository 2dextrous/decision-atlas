<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="Matching people and skills to demand, from the yearly headcount plan to tomorrow's roster." next="How it works" nextMinutes={3}>

Machines set a ceiling on capacity, but people often decide whether you reach it. Workforce planning works at two levels.

**Staffing (months ahead):** how many people with which skills. With seasonal demand, such as a sweets maker before Diwali or a warehouse during a sale season, there are three basic options:

- **Chase:** hire temporary staff for the peak and release them after. Capacity follows demand, but hiring and training cost money and new staff are slower.
- **Level:** keep a steady team and build stock in quiet months for the peak. Stable and skilled, but you carry inventory.
- **Mix:** a core team plus overtime, a few temporary workers and some pre-built stock. Most companies end up here.

**Shift scheduling (days and weeks ahead):** building rosters that cover the required hours. A warehouse might need 40 pickers from 6 pm to midnight during a sale but 15 on a normal Tuesday morning. Good rosters respect labour laws, maximum hours and rest days, and fairness, because people quit unfair rosters.

**Cross-training** makes everything easier: people who can work two or three stations let you move capacity to wherever the bottleneck is today.

Labour is also measured: units picked or produced per labour hour shows whether the plan is realistic and where training or layout changes would help.

<KeyIdea>

Plan the size of the team for the season, and plan the roster for the week. A flexible core team, with overtime, temps and cross-training, covers peaks better than hiring and firing.

</KeyIdea>

<Level n={2} title="How it works" minutes={3} teaser="Turning volume into headcount, planning a sale peak with core staff, overtime and temps, and allowing for absence." next="Deep dive" nextMinutes={4}>

### From volume to headcount

$$
\text{People needed} = \frac{\text{workload (units)}}{\text{units per person-hour} \times \text{hours per shift} \times \text{productive share}}
$$

The productive share allows for breaks, meetings, travel and waiting, which typically take a noticeable part of every shift.

<Example title="a warehouse sale">

Normal volume: 30,000 units picked a day. A trained picker averages 100 units an hour, and 85% of an 8-hour shift is productive, so each picker handles 680 units a day.

- Normal days: 30,000 / 680 = 44.1, so 45 pickers.
- Sale days: volume triples to 90,000, which would need 133 trained pickers.
- The plan: the 45 core pickers work 10-hour days (850 units each, 38,250 in total). Temporary staff, who manage about 70 units an hour in their first week (476 a day), cover the remaining 51,750: about 109 temps.
- Absence: if 8% of rostered people are absent on a typical day, 45 needed means about 49 on the roster.

</Example>

### Rostering: covering hours with shifts

Demand for people varies by hour: a warehouse might need 15 pickers in the morning and 40 in the evening during a sale. Rostering chooses how many people start each shift pattern so every hour is covered at the lowest cost, while respecting rules on maximum hours, rest days and breaks. Small cases fit in a spreadsheet; larger ones are solved as optimization problems by workforce scheduling tools.

### Measuring productivity

Track units per labour hour by activity (receiving, putaway, picking, packing), against engineered standards based on time studies where possible. The measure shows whether the plan is realistic, which areas need training or layout changes, and how quickly new staff are learning.

<KeyIdea>

Headcount comes from workload, productivity per hour and the productive share of each shift, plus an allowance for absence. Peaks are covered by a mix of core overtime and temporary staff, rostered hour by hour.

</KeyIdea>

</Level>

<Level n={3} title="Deep dive" minutes={4} teaser="Learning curves, cross-training chains, labour rules and fatigue, and why pure chase strategies cost more than they seem.">

### Learning curves

New workers start slowly and improve with repetition. A classic description is the **learning curve**: each time cumulative output doubles, the time per unit falls to a fixed percentage of what it was, such as 80% (first observed in aircraft production by Wright in 1936). For planning, this means temporary staff hired in the peak week deliver much less than trained staff; hiring a week early for training often pays for itself.

### Cross-training chains

Training everyone on every station is expensive. Research on flexibility (the same idea as Jordan and Graves's chains for plants and products) shows that a little cross-training, arranged as a chain, captures most of the benefit: if each person can work two stations and the skills link all stations in a loop, capacity can flow to wherever the bottleneck is today. A **skills matrix** showing who can do what, and at what level, makes this visible to supervisors.

### Labour rules, fatigue and safety

Local labour laws limit daily and weekly working hours, set rest periods and require higher pay for overtime. Beyond compliance, long shifts raise error and accident rates, so a plan that relies on weeks of 12-hour days is fragile. Peak plans should check legal limits, overtime cost and fatigue together.

### Why pure chase costs more than it seems

Hiring and releasing staff with every swing in demand looks efficient on paper, but it carries hidden costs:

- recruitment and training for every peak,
- low productivity and more errors while new staff learn,
- losing experienced people who don't want unstable work,
- reputation as an employer, which makes the next peak harder to staff.

That is why most companies run a stable core team and flex around it with overtime, temporary staff, cross-training and some pre-built stock.

### Planning across the year

Workforce plans connect to S&OP: the agreed volumes and inventory build decide the hours needed month by month, and the workforce plan in turn limits what supply can promise. Holidays, festivals and harvest seasons affect both demand and staff availability.

<Trap>

Planning headcount at 100% productivity, with no allowance for breaks, learning, absence or attrition, guarantees that the peak will be understaffed. Build each of those allowances in explicitly.

</Trap>

<KeyIdea>

New staff follow a learning curve, cross-training chains give cheap flexibility, and labour rules and fatigue cap overtime. A stable core plus planned flexibility usually beats pure chase once hidden costs are counted.

</KeyIdea>

<Q q="A warehouse's volume triples during a two-week sale. How would you plan labour?">

Forecast hourly volume by activity (receiving, picking, packing), convert it to labour hours using productivity rates, then cover the gap with overtime for the core team, temporary staff hired and trained early, cross-trained people moved to picking, and extended shifts. Also prepare by pre-picking or pre-packing best sellers where possible.

</Q>

<Q q="What are the risks of a pure chase strategy?">

Constant hiring and layoffs cost money, new staff are slower and make more errors, morale suffers, and in a tight labour market you may not find people when you need them. That's why most firms keep a core team and flex at the edges.

</Q>
