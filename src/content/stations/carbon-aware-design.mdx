<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="Putting emissions into the network model next to cost and service." soon>

A network designed only for cost often isn't the lowest-carbon one. Fewer, larger warehouses can mean longer truck journeys; air freight saves days and burns far more fuel than sea or rail. **Carbon-aware design** puts emissions into the same model as cost and service.

First, measure. Emissions are usually reported under the **GHG Protocol**:

- **Scope 1:** direct emissions, such as your own trucks and boilers.
- **Scope 2:** purchased electricity for plants and warehouses.
- **Scope 3:** everything else in the value chain, including outsourced transport, suppliers and product use. For most companies this is the largest share.

Freight emissions are commonly estimated as **tonne-kilometres × an emission factor** for each mode, which is far higher for air and road than for rail and sea.

Then design. There are two common ways to add carbon to the optimization:

- **As a cost:** put a price on each tonne of CO₂, so the model trades money against emissions directly.
- **As a constraint:** cap total emissions, or require a cut such as 20%, and find the cheapest network that meets it.

Running the model with different caps gives a **trade-off curve**: often the first chunk of emission cuts is cheap (better truck loads, shifting long lanes from road to rail, solar on warehouses), while deeper cuts cost much more. That curve is what leadership needs to set a sensible target.

<KeyIdea>

Measure emissions by scope, then put carbon into the network model as a price or a cap. The trade-off curve shows which cuts are cheap and which are expensive.

</KeyIdea>

I'd first measure emissions by scope, with freight estimated as tonne-kilometres times mode-specific emission factors, then add carbon to the network model either as a cost per tonne or as a cap. <Q q="How can you reduce transport emissions without redesigning the whole network?">

Fill trucks better, consolidate loads, shift long lanes from road to rail or coastal shipping, optimize routes, use cleaner vehicles and plan fewer rush shipments, which often go by air or part-empty trucks. Many of these also cut cost.

</Q>

<Q q="Why is Scope 3 hard to manage?">

It sits outside the company's direct control: suppliers, outsourced logistics and product use. Data comes from partners and is often estimated, so reducing it needs supplier engagement, better data sharing and procurement choices, not just internal changes.

</Q>
