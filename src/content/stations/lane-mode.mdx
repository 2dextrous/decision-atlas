<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="Every origin–destination pair gets its own best answer." next="How it works" nextMinutes={3}>

A **lane** is an origin–destination pair: Mumbai plant to Delhi warehouse, Pune plant to Bengaluru warehouse. Companies run hundreds of lanes, and each one can be served by different modes and carriers at different prices, speeds and reliability.

Lane optimization picks the best option for each lane, given its volume and service need:

| Option on Mumbai → Delhi | Cost per trip | Transit |
|---|---|---|
| Carrier A, road | ₹80,000 | 2 days |
| Carrier B, road | ₹75,000 | 3 days |
| Rail container + road ends | ₹62,000 | 4 days |

If the Delhi warehouse must be refilled within 2 days, only A works. If 4 days is fine and volumes are steady, rail saves money and carbon. But the extra days also cost something: each extra day in transit holds a day's flow as pipeline stock, and a less reliable mode needs more safety stock at the warehouse. For a high-value product that inventory cost can wipe out the freight saving.

Volume shapes the answer too. High, steady lanes justify dedicated trucks or rail contracts. Thin lanes are better served by part loads or by combining with nearby lanes. Many companies split a lane: a base volume goes by the cheap, slow mode, and urgent top-ups go by the fast one.

<KeyIdea>

Choose mode and carrier lane by lane on total cost: freight plus the inventory that slower or less reliable transit forces you to hold, within the service the lane needs.

</KeyIdea>

<Level n={2} title="How it works" minutes={3} teaser="The Mumbai–Delhi choice worked through with pipeline stock and safety stock, for a cheap product and an expensive one." next="Deep dive" nextMinutes={3}>

### Total cost per trip

For each option, add freight to the inventory cost the option creates:

- **Pipeline stock:** each extra day in transit holds the shipment's value for a day. Cost per trip = value × holding rate × extra days / 365.
- **Safety stock:** a less reliable mode needs a larger buffer at the destination, roughly $z \times d \times \sigma_L$ when lead-time variability dominates.

<Example title="rail or road, two products">

The Delhi warehouse receives half a truckload a day (182.5 trips a year), holding cost is 25% a year and $z$ = 1.65. Rail is ₹18,000 cheaper per trip than road carrier A, but takes 2 days longer, and its transit time varies more ($\sigma_L$ of 1 day against 0.3 days).

- Extra safety stock for rail: $1.65 \times 0.5 \times (1.0 - 0.3) \approx 0.58$ truckloads.

| Per trip | Detergent, ₹5 lakh a truckload | Smartphones, ₹2 crore a truckload |

Same lane, same trucks and trains, opposite answers: rail for detergent, road for phones.

### Split lanes

Many lanes are best served by two modes at once: the predictable base volume goes by the cheap, slow mode, and the variable or urgent part by the fast one. The slow mode's flow is planned well ahead; the fast mode absorbs surprises. This is the transport version of a base-and-surge strategy.

### Volume and service decide the rest

- **High, steady volume:** dedicated trucks, rail contracts or round trips.
- **Thin or irregular volume:** part loads, shared networks or combining with nearby lanes.
- **Tight service windows:** the fastest reliable option for that share of volume.

<KeyIdea>

Choose mode by lane on freight plus the pipeline and safety stock each option creates. Cheap, bulky products favour slow, cheap modes; valuable ones favour fast, reliable ones, and split lanes combine both.

</KeyIdea>

### Reliability can matter more than speed

Safety stock depends on the variability of lead time as much as on its length. A mode that takes 4 days, give or take a few hours, can need less buffer than one that averages 2 days but sometimes takes 5. Measure the distribution of transit times by lane and carrier, not just the average promised by the contract.

### Base and surge

Research on dual sourcing, such as Allon and Van Mieghem's "tailored base-surge" policy (2010), shows a simple structure works well: a slow, cheap source supplies a constant base rate, and a fast, expensive source responds to fluctuations. Applied to transport, the slow mode carries a steady flow sized below average demand, and the fast mode tops up when stock runs low. It captures most of the cost saving of the slow mode while keeping the responsiveness of the fast one.

### Peaks and capacity risk

In festive seasons and harvest periods, road capacity tightens and spot rates jump. Rail and long-term contracts can provide capacity when the spot market can't. Lane strategies should be tested against peak conditions: will contracted carriers actually accept loads, and what does the fallback cost?

### Carbon and policy

Mode choice is also the biggest lever for transport emissions, since rail and sea emit much less per tonne-kilometre than road and air. Dedicated freight corridors and better rail terminals are making rail more competitive in India, so lanes that were road-only a few years ago deserve a second look.

### Data that makes it honest

- Transit time distributions by lane, mode and carrier, from tracking data.
- Full cost per mode, including drayage, terminal handling and damage.
- Inventory value and holding rate by product.
- Volume patterns by lane, including seasonality.

<Trap>

Planning on contract transit times instead of measured ones hides the variability that drives safety stock. A mode that is "2 days" on paper and 2 to 5 days in practice needs the buffer of a 5-day mode.

</Trap>

<KeyIdea>

Reliability matters as much as speed, base-and-surge splits capture most of the savings of slow modes, peak capacity needs planning, and lane decisions must use measured transit times and full costs.

</KeyIdea>

<Q q="Rail is ₹18,000 cheaper per trip than road but two days slower. How do you decide?">

Add the inventory cost of two more days in transit, plus any extra safety stock for less reliable transit, and check whether the receiving warehouse's service promise still holds. For low-value, steady flows, rail usually wins; for high-value or urgent items, road may be cheaper overall.

</Q>

<Q q="What is a split-lane strategy?">

Moving the predictable base volume on a cheap, slower mode, and using a faster mode only for top-ups when demand spikes. It captures most of the freight saving while protecting service.
