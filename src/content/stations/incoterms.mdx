<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="Three-letter codes that settle costs and risk between buyer and seller." next="How it works" nextMinutes={3}>

When an Indian company buys machine parts from Germany, someone has to pay for trucking to the port, export clearance, the ocean freight, insurance, import duty and the last delivery, and someone carries the risk if the cargo is damaged on the way. **Incoterms**, published by the International Chamber of Commerce, are standard three-letter rules that settle exactly this. The current version is **Incoterms 2020**, with 11 rules:

| Rule | Seller delivers when… | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **EXW** Ex Works | goods are ready at the seller's premises | Buyer does almost everything |
| **FCA** Free Carrier | goods are handed to the buyer's carrier | Works for any mode, including containers |
| **FAS** Free Alongside Ship | goods are alongside the vessel | Sea only, bulk cargo |
| **FOB** Free on Board | goods are on board the vessel | Sea only; risk passes on loading |
| **CFR** Cost and Freight | goods are on board; seller pays freight | Risk passes on loading, cost later |
| **CIF** Cost, Insurance and Freight | as CFR, plus basic insurance | Sea only |
| **CPT** Carriage Paid To | goods go to the first carrier; seller pays carriage | Any mode |
| **CIP** Carriage and Insurance Paid To | as CPT, plus higher insurance | Any mode |
| **DAP** Delivered at Place | goods arrive, ready for unloading | Buyer pays import duty |
| **DPU** Delivered at Place Unloaded | goods are unloaded at destination | Replaced DAT in 2020 |
| **DDP** Delivered Duty Paid | goods arrive, import duty paid | Seller does almost everything |

The trap in the "C" terms: the seller pays freight to the destination, but **risk passes at origin**. Under CIF, if the ship sinks, the loss belongs to the buyer, who claims on the insurance.

Incoterms don't cover everything. They don't say when ownership passes, how payment works, or what happens if the contract is broken; the sales contract covers those.

<KeyIdea>

Each Incoterm defines the delivery point: where risk passes and which costs each side pays. Remember that for C terms, cost and risk split at different places.

</KeyIdea>

<Level n={2} title="How it works" minutes={3} teaser="Who pays for and bears the risk of each leg under the main terms, and a German machine-parts import costed four ways." next="Deep dive" nextMinutes={4}>

### Who does what

<div class="table-wrap">

| | FCA | FOB | CIF | DAP | DDP |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Export clearance | Seller | Seller | Seller | Seller | Seller |
| Main carriage paid by | Buyer | Buyer | Seller | Seller | Seller |
| Insurance | Buyer's choice | Buyer's choice | Seller (minimum cover) | Seller's choice | Seller's choice |
| Risk passes to the buyer | On handover to the buyer's carrier | Once on board the vessel | Once on board the vessel | On arrival, ready for unloading | On arrival, ready for unloading |
| Import clearance and duty | Buyer | Buyer | Buyer | Buyer | Seller |

</div>

Under CIF, the seller pays freight and insurance to the destination port, but risk passes when the goods are loaded at origin. Cost and risk split at different points; that is the defining feature of all four C terms.

### Costing the options

<Example title="machine parts from Germany to a plant near Pune">

The supplier quotes under four terms. The buyer's own costs for the legs it must arrange: export clearance and pre-carriage ₹25,000 (under EXW), ocean freight ₹80,000 through its forwarder, insurance ₹5,000, destination port charges ₹15,000, and the truck to the plant ₹30,000.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Term | Supplier's price | Buyer's other costs | Total before import duty |
|---|---|---|---|
| EXW | ₹10,00,000 | ₹1,55,000 | ₹11,55,000 |
| FCA | ₹10,20,000 | ₹1,30,000 | ₹11,50,000 |
| CIF Nhava Sheva | ₹11,10,000 | ₹45,000 | ₹11,55,000 |
| DAP buyer's plant | ₹12,00,000 | ₹5,000 (customs agent) | ₹12,05,000 |

</div>

FCA is cheapest here, because the buyer's forwarder has a better ocean rate than the seller charges for, while the seller handles German export formalities. DAP costs ₹55,000 more for the convenience of a delivered price.

</Example>

Import duty is calculated on the customs value, which in India includes freight and insurance to the port, so it is broadly the same whichever term is used.

<KeyIdea>

Each term fixes who pays for each leg and where risk passes. Compare terms on total landed cost, including your own freight and handling, and remember that C terms move cost to the seller without moving risk.

</KeyIdea>

<Level n={3} title="Deep dive" minutes={4} teaser="Using the right term for containers, what changed in 2020, letters of credit, customs valuation in India, and controlling your own freight.">

### Use container-friendly terms for containers

FOB, CFR and CIF were designed for goods loaded over a ship's side. Containers are handed over at a terminal days before loading, so under FOB the seller still carries risk while the box sits in a yard it no longer controls. The ICC recommends FCA, CPT and CIP for containerized cargo and for air, road and rail; the four sea terms (FAS, FOB, CFR, CIF) are meant for bulk and conventional cargo loaded directly onto ships.

### What changed in Incoterms 2020

- **DAT became DPU** (delivered at place unloaded), making clear that delivery can be at any place where goods are unloaded, not only a terminal.
- **FCA and bills of lading:** buyer and seller can agree that the buyer's carrier issues an on-board bill of lading to the seller, which banks often require under letters of credit.
- **Insurance levels:** CIP now requires broad (all-risks) cover, while CIF still requires only minimum cover.
- **Own transport:** the rules recognize that a party may use its own vehicles instead of a third-party carrier.

### Terms and payment

Incoterms decide delivery, costs and risk, not payment or ownership. Those come from the sales contract and the payment method. Under a **letter of credit**, the bank pays the seller against specified documents (often an on-board bill of lading), so the Incoterm, the transport documents and the credit's conditions must be consistent, or payment is delayed.

### Customs valuation in India

Indian customs values imports on a CIF basis: cost plus freight and insurance to the place of import. If a contract is on FOB or FCA terms, freight and insurance must be added; the customs valuation rules set default percentages when actual costs can't be established. The chosen term therefore affects the paperwork and the calculation, not whether freight is included.

### Controlling your own freight

Buying on F terms (FCA, FOB) gives the buyer control of the main carriage: choice of carrier, consolidation with other shipments, visibility and negotiating power. Buying on C or D terms is simpler but leaves freight costs inside the supplier's price and transport decisions in the supplier's hands. Large importers usually prefer F terms for regular flows and C or D terms for small or irregular ones.

<Trap>

Quoting FOB for an air shipment, or CIF for goods handed over at an inland container depot, uses sea-only terms where they don't fit, which leaves risk in an unclear place. Match the term to the mode and the real handover point.

</Trap>

<KeyIdea>

Use FCA, CPT or CIP for containers and non-sea modes, know the 2020 changes, align the term with letters of credit and documents, add freight and insurance for customs on F terms, and use F terms when controlling the main freight is worth it.

</KeyIdea>

<Q q="Under CIF, the ship sinks mid-ocean. Who bears the loss?">

The buyer. Under CIF, risk passes when the goods are loaded on board at the origin port, even though the seller paid the freight and insurance. The buyer claims on the insurance the seller arranged.

</Q>

<Q q="Why might a buyer prefer FCA or FOB over CIF?">

To control freight and insurance themselves: choose the carrier, negotiate rates, consolidate shipments from several suppliers and get better visibility. Under CIF the seller picks the carrier and the minimum insurance, which may not suit the buyer.

</Q>
