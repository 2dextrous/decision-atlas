<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="The building blocks of a forecast and the questions to ask before choosing a method." soon>

Factories, buyers and trucks all need time to react, so almost every supply chain decision is made against a forecast rather than real orders. A forecast is a best estimate of future demand, and every forecast is wrong. The useful questions are how wrong, and in which direction.

Most demand histories are a mix of a few patterns:

- **Level:** the typical amount sold right now.
- **Trend:** steady growth or decline, like a brand gaining shelf space.
- **Seasonality:** a pattern that repeats on the calendar, such as ice cream in summer, sweets before Diwali, or the weekend peak in a café.
- **Noise:** the leftover randomness that no model will explain.

Before choosing a method, start from the decision the forecast feeds, because that sets the horizon and the level of detail:

- Replenishing a warehouse needs weekly numbers per item and location, a few weeks to a few months ahead.
- Planning plant capacity needs monthly numbers per product family, several quarters ahead.
- Budgeting needs yearly numbers per category.

Forecasting at the wrong level gives a precise answer to the wrong question. A useful habit is to build the simplest possible forecast first, such as "next week = last week" (the **naive forecast**). Any method you adopt later has to beat it.

People also separate **forecasting** (a statistical estimate from data) from **demand planning** (the business process that adds market knowledge, promotions and judgment, then agrees one demand plan for supply to work from).

<Q q="What is the difference between forecasting and demand planning?">

Forecasting is the statistical estimate from history and drivers. Demand planning is the business process around it: it adds promotions, market intelligence and customer input, reviews the numbers with sales and marketing, and produces one agreed demand plan that supply and finance use.

</Q>

<Q q="How do you choose the horizon and granularity of a forecast?">

From the decision. Replenishment needs item-by-location weekly forecasts out to the lead time; capacity planning needs product-family monthly forecasts several quarters out; budgets need category-level annual numbers. Forecasting at the wrong level makes downstream decisions misaligned however good the model is.

</Q>
