<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="How to find dead stock early and decide what to do with it." next="How it works" nextMinutes={4}>

Every warehouse collects stock that has stopped selling: phone cases for a model nobody buys any more, last season's colours, spare parts for retired machines. Companies often call it **SLOB** (slow-moving and obsolete) or **E&O** (excess and obsolete).

The two words mean different things. **Slow-moving** stock still sells, just far slower than planned. **Obsolete** stock has no realistic future demand. Treating a slow mover as dead throws away value; treating dead stock as slow lets it lose value month after month.

Spotting it early is mostly arithmetic:

- **Ageing:** how long since the item last moved?
- **Cover:** how many months would current stock last at the current sales rate? 5,000 phone cases selling 20 a month is 250 months of cover, which is effectively obsolete.
- **Future demand:** is the product being phased out or replaced?

Then choose the exit that recovers the most: stop reordering, move the stock to locations where it still sells, discount or bundle it, return it to the supplier, rework it, sell it to a liquidator, or scrap it. Waiting is rarely free, because value usually drops the longer stock sits.

Finance cares too: when stock is unlikely to sell at its book value, companies set aside a provision or write it down, which hits profit. Good planning prevents the problem upstream with careful phase-out plans, smaller first orders for new or uncertain items, and forecasts that notice when demand is dying.

<KeyIdea>

Slow is not the same as dead. Measure ageing and cover, act early, and pick the exit that recovers the most value. Every month of waiting costs money.

</KeyIdea>

<Level n={2} title="How it works" minutes={4} teaser="Ageing buckets, cover and excess calculations, and the phone-case decision worked through in rupees." next="Deep dive" nextMinutes={4}>

### Measure it the same way every month

1. **Ageing:** days since the item last moved, in buckets such as 0–90, 91–180, 181–365 and over 365 days.
2. **Cover:** how many months the stock would last, using the *forecast* for coming months where you have one, not just last year's sales.
3. **Excess:** stock above what you can reasonably use, such as everything beyond 12 months of forecast plus safety stock.
4. **At risk:** items with a phase-out date, a replacement product or an engineering change, even if they still sell today.

### Decide on the economics, not the original cost

What you paid is gone: it is a sunk cost. The decision compares what each option recovers now with what keeping the stock will cost from here on.

<Example title="5,000 phone cases">

The cases cost ₹150 each and sell 20 a month. Holding cost is 25% of value a year.

- Keep 12 months of demand plus a small buffer, about 260 cases. The excess is 4,740 cases.
- Holding one case for a year costs 25% of ₹150 = ₹37.50.
- A liquidator offers ₹30 a case: 4,740 × ₹30 = ₹1,42,200 now.
- Keeping a case one more year costs more (₹37.50) than selling it today recovers (₹30), and its value will keep falling. Sell the excess now.

</Example>

The same arithmetic ranks the other exits: moving stock to a branch where it still sells, bundling it with a fast seller, returning it under a supplier agreement or reworking it, each net of handling and freight.

### The accounting side

Inventory is carried at the lower of cost and **net realisable value** (NRV): the expected selling price minus the costs of selling. This is the rule in IAS 2 and in India's Ind AS 2. If the excess cases will realistically fetch ₹30, their book value must come down from ₹150 to ₹30, a write-down of 4,740 × ₹120 = ₹5,68,800, which hits this period's profit. Many companies also book a general provision based on ageing, for example a rising percentage of value for stock older than six, twelve and twenty-four months.

<KeyIdea>

Track ageing, forward cover and excess every month. Decide exits on what you can recover now against what holding will cost from here, never on the original price, and expect finance to write stock down to its net realisable value.

</KeyIdea>

<Level n={3} title="Deep dive" minutes={4} teaser="Where dead stock comes from, how to stop it at the source, and the forecasting methods that notice decline.">

### Where dead stock comes from

- **Launch optimism:** first orders sized on hope, before any sales history exists.
- **Large minimums:** a supplier's minimum order or a long production run covers years of demand for a slow item.
- **Late phase-outs:** purchasing keeps buying an item that product management has already replaced.
- **Engineering changes:** a design change makes old parts unusable overnight.
- **Promotion leftovers** and **returns** that never get sorted back into sellable stock.
- **Incentives:** buyers rewarded for price discounts on large volumes, or sales teams inflating forecasts to secure supply.

### Prevention beats clearance

- **Phase-in and phase-out plans:** set a run-out date, stop replenishing once stock plus open orders covers expected demand to that date, and coordinate the switch to the replacement.
- **Smaller first buys** for new or uncertain items, with quick re-orders once demand shows up.
- **Negotiate minimums,** or pay a little more per unit for smaller lots on slow items; the holding saving often beats the price break.
- **Postponement and common components,** so less stock is committed to variants that may not sell.
- **Range reviews** that cut the tail of items selling little while adding complexity.

### Forecasts that notice decline

Standard methods are slow to see a dying product. Simple exponential smoothing keeps forecasting the old level for a while, and Croston's method updates its estimate only when a sale happens, so if sales stop entirely its forecast never falls. The **TSB method** (Teunter, Syntetos and Babai, 2011) updates the *probability* of demand every period instead, so a run of zeros steadily pulls the forecast down. That makes it well suited to items at risk of becoming obsolete.

### Who owns it

Slow and obsolete stock sits between planning, sales, product management and finance, so it often belongs to nobody. Good practice is a monthly review with a named owner, clear authority to dispose, and targets for both prevention (new excess created) and clearance (old excess removed).

<Trap>

A write-down is an accounting entry, not a disposal. The stock still takes space, costs handling and insurance, and keeps ageing. Equally, "it's written off, so keeping it is free" is wrong: holding costs continue until the stock physically leaves.

</Trap>

<KeyIdea>

Dead stock is mostly created upstream by launch optimism, large minimums and late phase-outs. Prevent it with run-out planning and small first buys, use forecasting methods like TSB that see decline, and give clearance a named owner.

</KeyIdea>

<Q q="You find 5,000 units that sell 20 a month. What do you do?">

That is 250 months of cover, effectively obsolete. Stop all replenishment immediately, check whether another region or channel still sells it, then compare the recovery from discounting, bundling, returning to the supplier or liquidating against the cost of keeping it. Provide for the expected loss in the books.

</Q>

<Q q="Why do standard forecasting methods struggle with dying products?">

Croston's method only updates its estimates when a sale happens, so when sales stop it keeps forecasting the old rate indefinitely. Simple exponential smoothing does drift down, but slowly. TSB updates the probability of demand every period, so its forecast falls steadily as the product dies and replenishment stops in time.

</Q>
