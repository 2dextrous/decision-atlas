<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="From a pile of orders to trucks on the road, step by step." soon>

Every day a distribution centre faces a pile of orders, each with a destination, a size and a due date. **Transport planning** turns that pile into trucks on the road at the lowest cost that still meets every delivery promise. It answers, in order:

1. **What ships together?** Group orders into shipments by destination and due date.
2. **How?** Choose the mode and vehicle size: full truck, part load, parcel.
3. **With whom?** Pick the carrier for each load from contracted rates, capacity and performance.
4. **How is it packed and routed?** Build loads that fit weight and volume limits; sequence stops on multi-drop routes.
5. **When?** Book pickup slots at the dock and delivery windows at customers.

Then execution: **tendering** the load to the carrier (who accepts or rejects), tracking it on the road, confirming delivery, and finally **freight audit and payment**, checking each invoice against the agreed rate.

A **TMS (transportation management system)** supports all of this: it holds carrier contracts and rates, optimizes loads and routes, sends tenders, collects tracking updates and audits invoices. The biggest savings usually come from planning across many orders at once, combining shipments and choosing modes, rather than from squeezing carrier rates.

The objective is roughly:

> minimize freight + accessorial charges (waiting, extra stops) + penalties for late delivery

subject to vehicle capacities, delivery windows, driver hours and carrier capacity.

Plan freight across many orders at once: consolidate, choose modes, assign carriers and route, all within service promises. A TMS turns that into a daily routine and checks every bill.

<Q q="What are the main functions of a TMS?">

Storing carrier contracts and rates, planning and optimizing shipments, loads and routes, tendering loads to carriers, tracking shipments and events, and auditing and paying freight invoices. Many also produce analytics on cost per shipment and carrier performance.

</Q>

<Q q="Where would you look for transport savings first?">

Consolidation and load utilization (fewer, fuller trucks), mode choice (part loads that could be full loads, or air that could be road), fewer emergency shipments caused by poor planning, and freight audit to catch billing errors. Rate renegotiation comes after the network and planning basics.

</Q>
