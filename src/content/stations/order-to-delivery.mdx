<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="The sell-side mirror of procurement, from order to cash in the bank." next="How it works" nextMinutes={3}>

When a supermarket chain orders 500 cases of shampoo, a chain of steps starts. Together they're called **order to cash (O2C)**, the sell-side mirror of procure-to-pay:

1. **Order capture:** the order arrives by EDI, a portal, a sales rep's app or email, and is checked for price, pack sizes and delivery date.
2. **Credit check:** is the customer within its credit limit?
3. **Availability check:** can we promise the quantity and date? (That's ATP.)
4. **Allocation:** if stock is short, who gets it?
5. **Delivery creation and picking:** the warehouse picks and packs the order.
6. **Goods issue and shipping:** stock leaves the building; ownership and cost of goods sold are booked.
7. **Proof of delivery:** the customer confirms receipt, quantities and condition.
8. **Billing and payment:** invoice, collection, and clearing the customer's account.

Things go wrong at every hand-off: a price mismatch blocks the order, a credit hold delays it, a picking error ships the wrong pack, a missed truck turns "on time" into "late". That's why companies measure the whole chain, not just the warehouse:

- **Order cycle time:** order received to order delivered.
- **OTIF:** share of orders delivered on time and in full.
- **Perfect order rate:** on time, complete, undamaged and with correct paperwork and invoice. Because it multiplies several conditions, it's always lower than any single measure.

<KeyIdea>

Order to delivery runs from capture through credit, availability, picking, shipping and proof of delivery to billing. Measure the whole chain, because customers feel the weakest hand-off.

</KeyIdea>

### Where the time goes

<Example title="one order's 53 hours">

<div class="table-wrap">

| Step | Hours | Mostly |
|---|---|---|
| Order capture and checks | 1 | Work |
| Credit check, including holds | 3 | Waiting |
| Availability check and allocation | 1 | Work |
| Waiting for the picking wave | 6 | Waiting |
| Picking and packing | 2 | Work |
| Waiting for the truck | 8 | Waiting |
| Transit | 30 | Moving |
| Unloading and proof of delivery | 2 | Work |

</div>

Of the 21 hours before the truck leaves, only about 4 are work; the rest is waiting for a credit release, a picking wave or a truck. Cutting those waits usually shortens the cycle more than speeding up any single task.

</Example>

### OTIF and the perfect order

**OTIF** counts an order as a success only if it arrives on time *and* complete. The **perfect order** adds more conditions: undamaged, with correct documents and a correct invoice. Because every condition must hold, the rate is roughly the product of the individual rates:

$$
0.96 \times 0.97 \times 0.99 \times 0.98 \approx 0.90
$$

Four measures that each look good (96% on time, 97% in full, 99% undamaged, 98% correct paperwork) give only about 90% perfect orders.

### Backward scheduling

To deliver on the requested date, the system works backwards: requested delivery date, minus transit time, gives the goods-issue date; minus loading time gives the loading date; minus picking and packing time gives the date the stock must be available. If stock won't be available by then, the system works forwards from the earliest availability date and proposes a new delivery date.

<KeyIdea>

Most order cycle time is waiting between steps, not work. OTIF and the perfect order multiply conditions, so they are always lower than any single measure. Backward scheduling from the requested date shows when each step must happen.

</KeyIdea>

### On time to what?

"On time" can be measured against the date the customer **requested** or the date the company **promised**. Measuring against the promised date flatters performance: if the company promises late dates, it can be "on time" while customers wait longer than they wanted. Track both, and treat the gap between requested and promised dates as a measure of its own.

### Designing order management

- **Order channels:** EDI (electronic data interchange) messages from large customers, portals and apps for smaller ones, each validated automatically against prices, pack sizes and delivery rules.
- **Credit management:** automatic release for low-risk customers and small amounts, with holds reviewed quickly; a credit hold that sits overnight can cost the delivery date.
- **Order prioritization:** when resources are short, rules decide which orders are picked and shipped first.
- **Backorders:** clear rules for partial shipments, backorders and cancellations, agreed with customers in advance.

### Differentiated service

Not every customer needs the same promise. Companies segment customers and set different delivery times, order cut-offs and minimum order sizes, priced into the commercial terms. A key retailer may get daily deliveries with tight windows; a small distributor, twice-weekly deliveries. Designing these promises deliberately, rather than serving everyone the same way, controls the cost to serve.

### When customers measure you

Large retailers score suppliers on OTIF at their own warehouses, often within narrow delivery windows, and charge penalties for misses. That moves the definition of "on time" into the customer's hands and makes accurate appointment booking, loading and transit as important as stock. Suppliers respond by measuring OTIF exactly as each key customer does, finding the causes of misses, and fixing the handful that dominate.

<Trap>

Reporting OTIF against promised dates, at line level and excluding "customer-caused" delays, can produce an impressive number that no customer recognizes. Measure the way your customers measure, starting from what they asked for.

</Trap>

<KeyIdea>

Measure delivery against the requested date as well as the promised one, design order management to remove waits and holds, differentiate service by segment deliberately, and align your OTIF definition with your key customers'.

</KeyIdea>

<Q q="OTIF is 95% but customers still complain. What might be wrong?">

OTIF may be measured against our promised date rather than the customer's requested date, or at line level rather than order level. Other failures don't show in OTIF at all: damage, wrong paperwork, invoice errors. The perfect order rate and the customer's own measurements give a truer picture.

</Q>

<Q q="Why is the perfect order rate always lower than OTIF?">

Because it requires every condition at once: on time, in full, undamaged and with correct documentation. If each is 95%, the combined rate is roughly 0.95 × 0.95 × 0.95 × 0.95, about 81%.
