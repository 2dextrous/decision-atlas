<Level n={1} title="The gist" minutes={3} teaser="When everyone can't have everything, the rule you choose shapes behaviour." next="How it works" nextMinutes={3}>

Sometimes there simply isn't enough: a raw material shortage, a plant outage or a demand spike. With 5,000 units available and orders for 9,000, someone gets less than they asked for. **Allocation** decides who, using an explicit rule rather than whoever shouts loudest:

- **First come, first served:** simple, but rewards early ordering, not importance.
- **Fair share:** everyone gets the same percentage, here 5,000 ÷ 9,000, about 56% of each order.
- **Priority tiers:** key accounts and contractual commitments first, then others.
- **Economic:** favour orders with the highest margin or the biggest penalty for missing them.
- **Optimized:** a model balancing margin, penalties, service targets and strategic value.

Customers A, B and C asking for 3,000, 2,000 and 4,000 might, under fair share, get about 1,700, 1,100 and 2,200. Under a priority rule where A has a contract, A might get its full 3,000 and B and C split the rest.

The choice changes behaviour. If customers learn that allocation is proportional to what they order, they inflate their orders to get more, a form of **shortage gaming**. That sends false demand signals upstream and is one of the classic causes of the bullwhip effect. Allocating on past sales history or on forecasts agreed in advance avoids rewarding inflated orders.

<KeyIdea>

Make allocation rules explicit and agree them in advance. Prefer rules based on history or agreed forecasts over current orders, so customers have no reason to inflate demand.

</KeyIdea>

### One shortage, four rules

<Example title="5,000 units, orders for 9,000">

Customers A, B and C order 3,000, 2,000 and 4,000. Over the last quarter they bought 40%, 35% and 25% of the volume. A has a supply contract.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Rule | A | B | C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fair share of orders (56% each) | 1,667 | 1,111 | 2,222 |
| Share of past sales (40/35/25%) | 2,000 | 1,750 | 1,250 |
| Contract first, then fair share | 3,000 | 667 | 1,333 |

</div>

C normally buys a quarter of the volume but ordered 44% of the total this time. Under fair share of orders, C gets 2,222 units; under past sales, 1,250. If C inflated its order expecting rationing, the fair-share rule rewarded it, and next time everyone will inflate.

</Example>

A fourth approach, **economic** allocation, ranks orders by margin or by the penalty for missing them. It maximizes short-term profit but can damage relationships with customers who are less profitable today and important tomorrow.

### Allocation quotas set in advance

Allocation works best as a planned process, not an emergency decision:

1. **Forecast the shortage:** S&OP shows supply below demand for certain products and weeks.
2. **Set quotas** per customer, channel or region, based on agreed forecasts, history or contracts.
3. **Load the quotas into order promising,** so each order consumes its group's allocation.
4. **Release unused quotas** at an agreed point before shipment, so stock isn't stranded.
5. **Review weekly** as supply and demand change.

<KeyIdea>

The allocation rule decides who gets what and how customers behave next time. Allocating on past sales or agreed forecasts discourages inflated orders, and quotas set in advance and loaded into promising make allocation predictable.

</KeyIdea>

### Allocation as an optimization

With many products, customers and constraints, allocation can be modelled: maximize margin minus penalties for unmet commitments, subject to supply, minimum service levels for strategic customers, contractual quantities and fairness rules such as no customer below a certain share of its forecast. The model makes trade-offs explicit, and planners can see what each rule costs.

### Turn-and-earn and gaming

Cachon and Lariviere (1999) studied allocation based on past sales, known in the car industry as **turn-and-earn**: dealers that sell more today earn a larger share of scarce models later. It discourages inflated orders and rewards real sales, but it can push retailers to sell harder (with discounts) in slow periods to secure future allocation. Their broader work shows that allocation rules shape the orders customers place: rules that reward order size invite inflation, which feeds the bullwhip effect.

### Contract terms that matter

- **Allocation clauses:** how the supplier will share scarce output among customers.
- **Firm commitments:** volumes the supplier must deliver regardless, often in return for the buyer's commitment.
- **Force majeure:** what counts, and how obligations change when it applies.
- **Price during shortages:** whether prices can rise and how.

Knowing these terms before a shortage avoids disputes during one.

### Communicating a shortage

Customers cope better with a shortage they hear about early and clearly:

- Tell them as soon as the shortage is known, with the expected duration.
- Explain the rule and apply it consistently.
- Give each customer their quantity and dates, and update them when anything changes.
- Offer alternatives: substitutes, other pack sizes, later dates.

Customers who trust the process are less likely to inflate orders, which keeps the demand signal honest.

<Trap>

Deciding allocation case by case, under pressure from whichever sales manager calls loudest, creates inconsistent treatment and teaches customers to escalate and inflate. Agree the rules before the shortage and apply them through the system.

</Trap>

<KeyIdea>

Model allocation when stakes and complexity are high, choose rules that don't reward inflated orders, know the contract terms that govern shortages, and communicate early and consistently.

</KeyIdea>

<Q q="5,000 units are available and three customers order 3,000, 2,000 and 4,000. How would you allocate?">

First check commitments and priorities: does any customer have a contract or strategic importance? Without them, fair share gives about 56% each: roughly 1,700, 1,100 and 2,200. With priorities, serve the committed customer first and share the rest. Then tell customers clearly what they'll get and when the shortage should end.

</Q>

<Q q="Why can allocating in proportion to orders make shortages worse?">

Customers learn to inflate orders to get a bigger share. The supplier then sees phantom demand, may over-expand or over-produce, and when the shortage ends, orders collapse as customers cancel the extras. This is shortage gaming, a known cause of the bullwhip effect.

</Q>
